The first E&I class, with Prof Vandita Dar, argued that you learn more from failure than success. It used Uber to show seeing a problem differently, James Dyson to show persistence, and product exercises (the iPhone launch, a juice machine, a transport product) to test ideas before money is spent.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning | Class example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Entrepreneur | Someone who starts a venture to solve a problem, taking the risk | Travis Kalanick, Uber |
| Intrapreneur | Someone who acts like a founder inside an existing company | Thinking like a founder as an employee |
| Startup | A new venture bringing real innovation to a market | Uber, Airbnb |
| Execution | Turning an idea into a working business | "Anyone can copy an idea, few can build it" |
| Product-market fit | A product that the right customers want enough to pay for | A good product still needs the right customer |
| Substitute | Another product that meets the same need | Rating a new transport product against alternatives |

## Learning from failure

**Key idea:** Study failure more than success. Big, established companies often fail when they expand into a crowded market with no clear advantage.
**Class example:** A good product is not enough. You must find the right customer.
**Business example:** Starbucks entered Australia in 2000 and in 2008 closed 61 of its 84 stores. Australians already had a strong local cafe culture, so Starbucks had no clear advantage there.
**Exam tip:** In a failure question, name the cause precisely: wrong customer, no advantage, poor timing or weak execution. Then give a company that failed for that reason.

## Intrapreneurship

**Definition:** Intrapreneurship means thinking and acting like a founder inside an existing company, using its resources to build something new.
**Class example:** It matters even if you never start your own firm.
**Business example:** 3M's Post-it Notes came from employees Spencer Silver and Art Fry using company time to develop a weak adhesive into a new product. Gmail began as an internal Google project by engineer Paul Buchheit.

## Case: Uber, seeing things differently

**Definition:** Opportunity recognition is noticing a problem others accept as normal and designing a better way.
**Class example:** Travis Kalanick could not get a cab in Paris even with a credit card in hand. Uber built a simple app to connect riders with drivers, and recruited drivers by asking them to earn in their idle time.
**Business example:** Uber was founded in 2009 by Garrett Camp and Travis Kalanick. In India, Ola started in 2010 and adapted the same idea with auto-rickshaws and cash payment.
**Key idea:** Class debate added that execution matters more than the idea, because an idea can be copied but a working operation is hard to build.

## What stops people starting a business

1. Fear of failing at execution.
2. Doubt about whether the idea is good enough.
3. Difficulty finding customers.
4. Money running out before the business works.
5. The time it takes.
6. The need for family support and a network.

**Remember:** Most of these barriers are about uncertainty and support, not about the idea itself.

## Persistence

**Class example:** James Dyson built thousands of prototypes before his vacuum worked. Money helps, but the drive to keep going is personal.
**Business example:** Dyson made 5,127 prototypes over about five years before the bagless cyclone vacuum worked. Dyson is now a global appliance brand.
**Exam tip:** Use Dyson as the standard example of persistence and iteration.

## What counts as a startup

**Definition:** A startup brings real innovation to a market and aims to grow fast. Opening an ordinary business or continuing a family business is not the same.
**Class example:** MBA Chai Wala was the grey-area example. Anant Ambani, Anand Mahindra and the Adani Group were discussed as corporate, not startup, examples.
**Business example:** Prafull Billore started MBA Chai Wala in 2017 as a tea stall and grew it into a franchise chain. Critics argue it is a strong small business brand rather than a tech startup.

## Product evaluation exercises

### The iPhone launch

**Class example:** In 2007 Steve Jobs presented the iPhone as three products (a touchscreen iPod, a phone and an internet communicator) and repeated the list to build suspense before revealing they were one device.
**Key idea:** How you present a product shapes how people value it.

### The juice machine

**Class example:** A juice machine cost about $700 and raised about $120 million, including from Google Ventures. The juice came in packets, so it did not feel fresh, and the packs looked like IV bags.
**Business example:** This was Juicero, launched in 2016. After Bloomberg reported in 2017 that its packs could be squeezed by hand, the company shut down within months. Heavy funding could not fix a product people did not need.

### The transport product

**Class example:** Students rated a new short-distance transport product: its closest substitute and a fair price.
**Exam tip:** Evaluate any product idea on four questions: the problem, the target customer, the closest substitute and the price the customer would pay.

### Rental models

**Class example:** As hotel demand rises, owners rent out houses and resorts to specific customer groups.
**Business example:** Airbnb began in 2008 when its founders rented air mattresses in their San Francisco flat. It grew into a global platform without owning property.

## What the faculty stressed

- Learn from failure first.
- A good product needs the right customer.
- Execution beats ideas.
- Intrapreneurship matters for every employee.

## Practice questions

**Q1.** Distinguish an entrepreneur from an intrapreneur with examples.
**A:** An entrepreneur starts a new venture and carries the risk, such as Travis Kalanick with Uber. An intrapreneur builds something new inside an existing company, such as the 3M employees who developed Post-it Notes.

**Q2.** What lesson does Juicero teach about funding and product need?
**A:** Juicero raised about $120 million but its packs could be squeezed by hand, so the machine added little value. Funding cannot replace a real customer need, and the company shut down in 2017.

**Q3.** Why did Starbucks struggle in Australia?
**A:** Australia already had a strong local cafe culture, so Starbucks offered no clear advantage. It closed 61 of 84 stores in 2008, showing that expanding into a crowded market without an edge leads to failure.

**Q4.** Explain why execution matters more than the idea, using Uber and Ola.
**A:** The ride-hailing idea was easy to copy, as Ola showed in India. What decided success was execution: building driver supply, local payment options and reliable service. A copied idea with better execution can win locally.

**Q5.** List four questions for evaluating a new product idea.
**A:** What problem does it solve, who exactly is the customer, what is the closest substitute, and what price will the customer pay compared with that substitute?

**Q6.** Why is Dyson a strong example of entrepreneurial persistence?
**A:** James Dyson built 5,127 prototypes over about five years before the bagless vacuum worked. His persistence turned repeated failure into a global brand.
