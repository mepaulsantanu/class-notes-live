Prof Sachin Sinha used this class to look at how every element of the marketing mix communicates, using the VTP (Visual, Tactile, Performance) scheme, price equations, place cues and the promotion toolkit. The second part moved to advertising itself: its philosophy, its tricks (converting the mundane to the magical, puffery), product identification through packaging and labeling, regulation by ASCI, and the formal AMA definition. The afternoon session analysed advertising appeals through Snickers and a "missing person" garment ad, and worked through the Incredible India campaign as a full case of global tourism communication.

## Communication through the marketing mix

### Every P communicates

**Definition:** Each of the four Ps (product, price, place, promotion) sends a message to the customer, so each has a "communication version". Promotion is only the most visible one.
**Class example:** The professor showed a diagram mapping the communication version of all four elements of the marketing mix.
**Business example:** Maruti Suzuki runs separate Arena and Nexa showrooms. The place itself tells you which cars are premium before any salesperson speaks.
**Exam tip:** When asked about IMC, show that communication is wider than advertising. Product design, price and outlet all carry messages.

### The traditional promotion toolkit

**Definition:** The five classic promotion tools are advertising, publicity, public relations, direct marketing and personal selling.
**Class example:** Listed while reviewing the 4 Ps diagram; sales promotion was discussed separately later in class.
**Business example:** A car launch by Tata Motors typically combines TV advertising, press coverage (publicity), a launch event (PR), SMS and email offers (direct marketing) and dealership sales staff (personal selling).
**Remember:** Five tools: A, P, PR, DM, PS.

### Brand recovery through communication

**Definition:** After a negative event, a brand uses well-produced communication to rebuild customer confidence.
**Class example:** After the Fly Dubai aircraft incident involving an Indian pilot, the airline released a series of high-quality advertisements to restore trust in its services.
**Business example:** After the 2003 worm controversy, Cadbury India ran "Project Vishwas" and brought in Amitabh Bachchan for reassurance ads, along with new sealed packaging. Nestle's Maggi ran a "We miss you too" style comeback campaign after its 2015 ban.

## The VTP communication scheme

### Visual (V)

**Definition:** Unspoken communication from the appearance and look of a good before purchase.
**Class example:** The first look of a garment on the rack. In services, students often choose a university based on the campus ambiance.
**Business example:** Tanishq showrooms use lighting, display and interiors to signal trust and premium quality the moment you walk in.

### Tactile (T)

**Definition:** Communication through touch and feel, often used to judge quality.
**Class example:** Feeling the fabric of a garment (a habit more common among older generations). In cars, the physical comfort of the seat and the leg space. In services, tactile communication is often inconsistent: what you feel during admission can differ from the experience after enrolment.
**Business example:** Raymond stores let you handle fabric bolts before ordering a suit, because feel is a key quality signal for suiting.

### Performance (P)

**Definition:** Communication based on how the product actually works or fits when used, the "try out" phase.
**Class example:** Trial rooms for clothes, test drives for cars, fittings for tailored bridal wear, a fruit vendor offering a grape or cutting a piece of fruit for you to taste. In services, coaching centres run demo classes so students can trial before paying.
**Business example:** Lenskart's home try-on and in-store trials let you check frames on your face before buying. Car brands such as Hyundai and Tata Motors offer home test drives.

### VTP across product categories

**Definition:** The relative weight of V, T and P changes by category.
**Class example:** Garments use all three. Automobiles rely heavily on tactile comfort and performance (test drive). For fruits and vegetables, visual appeal may matter less than touch and taste.
**Business example:** FMCG food brands use in-store sampling at retail chains like DMart and Reliance Smart because taste (performance) convinces more than the pack.
**Exam tip:** Apply VTP to both a good and a service in your answer. The class covered services (university, coaching) as well as goods.

## Price communication

### Price-Quality (PQ) equation

**Definition:** The general assumption that a higher price signals higher quality.
**Class example:** Discussed as the most common price equation in consumer minds.
**Business example:** Premium mattress and appliance brands often price above the market so buyers infer better quality.

### Price-Technology (PT) equation

**Definition:** The assumption that a higher price means more advanced technology.
**Class example:** Higher-priced products are associated with newer technology.
**Business example:** Dyson prices its vacuum cleaners and hair dryers far above Indian rivals, and buyers read the price as a signal of superior engineering.

### Price-Status (PS) equation

**Definition:** High-value purchases are used to project social status.
**Class example:** Linked directly to conspicuous consumption by the Indian middle class.
**Business example:** Many Indian buyers take an iPhone on EMI partly because the brand signals status.

### Conspicuous consumption

**Definition:** Buying luxury or expensive products mainly to display status in society. The term was coined by Thorstein Veblen in *The Theory of the Leisure Class* (1899) (textbook).
**Class example:** The professor linked this urge in the Indian middle class to the colonial past: being ruled by the British left a lingering low self-esteem that consumers try to compensate for through material display. The word "conspicuous" has biological roots, referring to the bright colours of flower petals that attract bees for pollination. The class debated whether Gen Z still behaves this way or whether it was stronger in earlier generations.
**Business example:** Luxury car brands like Mercedes-Benz and BMW, and big fat Indian weddings, are classic Indian cases of status display.
**Remember:** Conspicuous = designed to be noticed, like a petal attracting a bee.

### Reasonable price

**Definition:** Consumers carry a mental price range with a lower and an upper limit. A price above the upper limit is too costly; a price below the lower limit makes them doubt the quality and reject the product.
**Class example:** Discussed as the reason "too cheap" can fail.
**Business example:** The Tata Nano was marketed as the "cheapest car", and the tag put off buyers who did not want to be seen in a cheap car. Ratan Tata later admitted the "cheapest car" label was a mistake.
**Exam tip:** Link reasonable price to the PQ equation. Low price can send a negative quality message.

## Place communication

**Definition:** The point of purchase itself acts as a marketing and communication tool.
**Class example:** Branded carry bags, such as Big Bazaar bags, once served as fashion statements and objects of prestige for Indian consumers. The professor referred to writer Rama Pija worker [Rama Bijapurkar], who writes about the unique and often ironic nature of the Indian consumer.
**Business example:** Shoppers reuse premium store bags from brands like Zara or Apple, so the bag keeps advertising the store after the sale.
**Remember:** Rama Bijapurkar's well-known book on Indian consumers is *We Are Like That Only* (textbook).

## Sales promotion

**Definition:** Incentivising purchase through discounts and special schemes for a limited period.
**Class example:** In India, sales promotion is often culturally endorsed. Dhanteras encourages buying something new (traditionally steel utensils), so the festival acts as a cultural driver for sales.
**Business example:** Jewellers such as Tanishq run Dhanteras offers on making charges, and Flipkart's Big Billion Days and Amazon's Great Indian Festival are timed around Diwali.
**Exam tip:** Mention cultural endorsement as an India-specific feature of sales promotion.

## Publicity and public relations

**Definition:** Activities that build visibility and goodwill, ranging from high-end events to low-cost distribution of material.
**Class example:**
- High-end: the Auto Expo for vehicles, Apple's annual Keynote events for iPhone launches.
- Low-cost: leaflets, pamphlets and business cards handed out at malls.
- Innovative: for an Aamir Khan film (likely *Ghajini*, 2008), multiplex staff sported the film's haircut to create a cultural connection.
- Outdoor: billboards and political-style road shows.
**Business example:** Reliance announces major Jio launches at its Annual General Meeting, which generates huge free media coverage.

## Experiential marketing

**Definition:** Creating an immersive experience around the product so the customer feels it, not just sees it.
**Class example:** A horror film (named in class as Kaalo / Vastu Shastra) set up cinema lobbies with black magic themes, artificial fire and ushers dressed as ghosts. Reliance Jio's launch used a high-profile Mumbai event with Aamir Khan, Shah Rukh Khan and Sachin Tendulkar to build massive visibility.
**Business example:** Car brands set up off-road test tracks at the Auto Expo so visitors experience the vehicle's performance first hand.

## Product identification: branding, packaging and labeling

### Packaging

**Definition:** Packaging is of two main types. Protective packaging focuses on safety and preservation. Aesthetic packaging focuses on decoration, convenience and visual appeal.
**Class example:** Discussed as part of product identification alongside branding and labeling.
**Business example:** Amul's Tetra Pak milk is protective packaging that keeps milk safe for months. Cadbury Celebrations gift boxes are aesthetic packaging built for festive gifting.

### Grade labeling

**Definition:** Labels that certify grade, standard or category.
**Class example:** Barcodes (Universal Product Code, UPC), red and green dots for non-veg and veg, FSSAI licence, ISI mark, Hallmark for jewellery.
**Business example:** BIS hallmarking of gold jewellery became mandatory in India in 2021, so every Tanishq or Kalyan piece carries the hallmark.

### Descriptive labeling

**Definition:** Labels that describe the product's details.
**Class example:** Expiry date, manufacturing and packing dates, ingredients, manufacturer address, and regional sales restrictions (for example, books "for sale in India, Nepal, Bhutan, Bangladesh and Sri Lanka only").
**Business example:** A Maggi noodles pack lists ingredients, nutrition facts, MRP, best-before date and Nestle India's address.
**Exam tip:** Grade = certifies standard. Descriptive = tells you facts about the product. Give two examples of each.

## The philosophy of advertising

### What advertising is

**Definition:** The professor described advertising as the science of arresting human intelligence long enough to make money, often by turning mundane experiences into magical ones through illusion.
**Class example:** Advertising works like a magic show: it suspends the customer's natural intelligence and rational thinking.
**Business example:** Cadbury Dairy Milk's "Kuch Meetha Ho Jaye" turned eating a chocolate into a celebration ritual.

### Etymology

**Definition:** "Advertise" comes from the Latin word advertia [advertere], meaning to turn one's attention towards something.
**Class example:** Used to explain that the first job of an ad is to capture attention.
**Business example:** Amul's topical hoardings grab attention by commenting on current news.

### Positioning

**Definition:** Creating a specific value spot or space for the brand in the customer's mind. The idea was popularised by Al Ries and Jack Trout (textbook).
**Class example:** Discussed as the purpose behind advertising messages.
**Business example:** Fevicol owns "strong bond" in Indian minds through its humorous ads.

### Mesmerising the audience

**Definition:** Being so overwhelmed by oratory or performance that you lose your sense of right and wrong. The word comes from Lord Mesmer [Franz Anton Mesmer, an 18th-century German physician known for "animal magnetism"].
**Class example:** Ads aim to mesmerise viewers the way a magician does.
**Business example:** Celebrity-heavy launch films hold viewers through star power more than product facts.

## Converting the mundane to the magical

**Definition:** Advertising takes ordinary, repetitive tasks and shows them as joyful or magical experiences.
**Class example:**
- Detergent and dishwashing ads showing people cherishing stain removal.
- Christ University promotional films showing students and teachers far more active and enthusiastic than in daily classes.
- Pen ads showing students enjoying writing during an exam.
- Home appliance ads showing cooking and cleaning as an overflow of love rather than daily drudgery.
**Business example:** Surf Excel's "Daag Achhe Hain" turned dirty clothes into a sign of a child's good deeds.

## Puffery: the "legalized lie"

**Definition:** Exaggeration in advertising that is not meant to be taken as a factual claim, so it cannot easily be challenged in court.
**Class example:**
- Shampoo ads promising impossible shine, strength or length.
- Toothpaste ads with spotless white teeth and perfect gums.
- Real estate ads near the Buddh International Circuit (Formula 1 track) showing residents watching races from their balconies, which is physically impossible.
- Flipkart Big Billion Day ads using an excessive number of celebrities and long durations to build hype.
**Business example:** Red Bull's "Red Bull gives you wings" is a famous puffery line; Thums Up's "Taste the Thunder" works the same way.
**Remember:** Puffery is vague and subjective. A specific, measurable false claim is misleading advertising, not puffery.

## Regulation and ethics

**Definition:** The Advertising Standards Council of India (ASCI) is a self-regulatory watchdog that checks whether ads follow ethical norms. It is a watchdog, not a law enforcement body.
**Class example:** Compared with sector regulators IRDA [IRDAI] (insurance), TRAI (telecom) and SEBI (securities). Cases mentioned: the legal dispute between Dabur Honey and Patanjali over claims of authenticity and purity, and Patanjali's claims of a corona cure.
**Business example:** Patanjali's Coronil was promoted as a COVID cure; the AYUSH Ministry asked it to stop such claims, and in 2024 the Supreme Court pulled up Patanjali over misleading ads. ASCI's 2021 influencer guidelines require disclosure tags such as #ad.
**Exam tip:** Distinguish ASCI (self-regulation, advisory) from statutory regulators (IRDAI, TRAI, SEBI) that have legal powers.

## Formal definition of advertising (AMA)

**Definition:** The American Marketing Association defines advertising as a paid form of non-personal presentation of goods, services and ideas by an identified sponsor.
**Class example:**
- Paid: advertising cannot be free, because you must buy space or airtime in a medium (newspaper, social media, TV, radio).
- Non-personal: it is mass communication aimed at the public, with no one-to-one interaction between sponsor and receiver.
**Business example:** HUL buys TV airtime during IPL matches for Surf Excel; HUL is the identified sponsor, the message is paid and goes to millions at once.
**Remember:** Four parts: paid, non-personal, goods/services/ideas, identified sponsor. Publicity fails the "paid" test; personal selling fails the "non-personal" test.

## Advertising appeals

### What an appeal is

**Definition:** An appeal is the central approach an ad uses to attract attention and move the audience, such as humour, fear, emotion or curiosity. Appeals are usually grouped as rational or emotional (textbook).
**Class example:** The professor said the mid-term and end-term exams will focus heavily on appeals, so you should be able to name the appeal in any ad and explain how it works.
**Business example:** Fevicol uses humour, Dettol uses fear of germs, and Cadbury's festive ads use warmth and family emotion.
**Exam tip:** Use drawings and doodles to explain an ad in your answer script. The professor encourages visuals over long text.

### Humour appeal: Snickers

**Definition:** A humour appeal uses comedy to make the message memorable and likeable.
**Class example:** Snickers ads show that hunger makes people act abnormally, for example claiming to love Mondays. Eating a Snickers restores their normal self.
**Business example:** Mars' global "You're not you when you're hungry" campaign was adapted in India with characters turning back into themselves after a bite.
**Remember:** Snickers formula: hunger = not yourself; Snickers = normal again.

### Curiosity and alarm: the "missing" garment ad

**Definition:** An ad that copies a familiar format, such as a public notice, to create alarm and curiosity so the reader stops and reads.
**Class example:** A garment store ad was designed like a public notice for a missing person, with a "missing" headline. Its content compared choosing a bride with choosing a wedding outfit.
**Business example:** Amul's topical hoardings borrow news headlines in a similar way to grab instant attention.

### Ethics of deceptive formats

**Definition:** Creative ads can cross into deception when they imitate official or serious formats or make offensive comparisons.
**Class example:** The class debated whether copying a missing person notice to sell clothes is unethical or simply creative. The bride vs outfit comparison was flagged as absurd and possibly offensive.
**Business example:** ASCI regularly upholds complaints against ads found to mislead or to demean women.
**Exam tip:** In appeal questions, add one line on ethics. It shows judgement, and the class discussed it directly.

## Case study: the Incredible India campaign

### Origins and objective

**Definition:** A national tourism campaign that brings a whole country under one brand to attract foreign visitors.
**Class example:**
- Launched in 2002 by the Ministry of Tourism, Government of India.
- The timing followed the 9/11 attacks of 2001, which had hit global travel and India's tourist arrivals.
- It united the state tourism boards under one banner to create a single image of India.
- "Atithi Devo Bhava" (the guest is God) was part of the same effort to welcome foreign guests.
**Business example:** Kerala Tourism's "God's Own Country" ran as a strong state brand, and Incredible India gave such state efforts one national umbrella.
**Remember:** 2002, Ministry of Tourism, post 9/11, one banner for all states.

### Market-specific execution

**Definition:** Adapting the creative message to each target city while keeping one campaign identity.
**Class example:**
- London: electronic posters on buses with yoga practitioners and the line "go back to 3000 BC and get a healthier life", positioning India as the home of ancient wellness.
- Paris: magazines in executive lounges at Charles de Gaulle Airport showing temples, mosques and churches with the line "God created man, and man is returning the favour by building houses for God."
- New York: LED displays in Times Square on Valentine's Day Eve (13 February) showing the Taj Mahal, a sarcastic dig at flowers and chocolates compared with the Taj as a gift of love.
**Business example:** McDonald's India builds local ads around the McAloo Tikki while keeping the global brand, the same "one brand, local message" logic.
**Exam tip:** Match each city to its appeal: London = health, Paris = spirituality and architecture, New York = love and romance.

### Media and partnerships

**Definition:** Choosing media that reach the exact target audience and adding partners who make travel easier.
**Class example:**
- Niche magazines such as Architectural Digest, Overdrive and Inside Outside to reach high-society readers.
- BBC, CNN and National Geographic for European markets.
- The website was translated into several European languages.
- Visa and American Express tie-ups offered cash-back and discounts to tourists.
**Business example:** Taj Hotels partners with credit card issuers and luxury travel magazines to reach affluent international travellers.

### Crisis management and cultural promotion

**Definition:** Using communication to restore confidence after a disaster and to sell the country's culture as an experience.
**Class example:** After the December 2004 tsunami hit coastal areas and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, the government worked to restore tourist confidence. In Berlin, the campaign staged mock marriages to tap global interest in the "Big Fat Indian Wedding".
**Business example:** Kerala Tourism ran campaigns to bring tourists back after the 2018 floods, presenting the state as safe and open again.
**Remember:** The campaign responded to two crises: 9/11 and the 2004 tsunami.

## Practice questions

**Q1.** Explain the VTP communication scheme with one example each from a product and a service.
**A:** VTP stands for Visual, Tactile and Performance cues. For a garment, visual is its look on the rack, tactile is the feel of the fabric, and performance is the fit in the trial room. For a coaching centre, visual is the classroom ambiance, tactile is the feel of the admission process, and performance is a free demo class before you pay.

**Q2.** What are the PQ, PT and PS equations in price communication?
**A:** The Price-Quality equation is the belief that a higher price means higher quality. The Price-Technology equation links higher price with more advanced technology, as with Dyson appliances. The Price-Status equation is the use of expensive purchases to project social status, which drives conspicuous consumption.

**Q3.** What is puffery? Why is it called a "legalized lie"?
**A:** Puffery is exaggeration in ads that is not meant to be taken as a factual claim, such as shampoo promising impossible shine or real estate ads showing F1 races from balconies near the Buddh International Circuit. Since no reasonable person treats it as fact, it is hard to challenge in court. ASCI steps in when exaggeration becomes a misleading factual claim, as with Patanjali's corona cure claims.

**Q4.** Analyse the Snickers advertising campaign in terms of its appeal.
**A:** Snickers uses a humour appeal. Its ads show that hunger makes people act unlike themselves, such as claiming to love Mondays, and a bite of Snickers brings them back to normal. The humour makes the ad memorable and ties the product to one clear benefit: it fixes hunger.

**Q5.** Define advertising as per the AMA and explain its key components.
**A:** The AMA defines advertising as a paid form of non-personal presentation of goods, services and ideas by an identified sponsor. It is paid because the sponsor must buy space or airtime in a medium. It is non-personal because it is mass communication with no one-to-one interaction, and the sponsor is identified, which separates it from publicity.

**Q6.** How did the Incredible India campaign adapt its message to different global markets?
**A:** Launched in 2002 after 9/11 hit travel, the campaign put all states under one banner but changed its creative by city. London buses showed yoga with "go back to 3000 BC and get a healthier life", Paris airport lounges carried temples, mosques and churches, and Times Square showed the Taj Mahal on Valentine's Day Eve. Niche magazines, BBC, CNN, National Geographic and Visa and American Express tie-ups supported the message.
