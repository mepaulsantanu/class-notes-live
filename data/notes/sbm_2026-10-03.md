This class introduced the brand prism model and placed it next to David Aaker's and Kapferer's ideas of brand identity. Most of the session went into the brand mantra: emotional modifiers (positive and negative), descriptive modifiers, and why a mantra must stay the same across markets and joint ventures. Groups then presented positioning work on an affordable tech brand and an outdoor sports brand, and the faculty gave feedback on how to state the value to the customer.

## Brand identity models

### Brand Identity Prism (Kapferer)

**Definition:** Jean-Noël Kapferer's prism describes brand identity using six facets. Physique, relationship and reflection are the outward (externalised) facets. Personality, culture and self-image are the inward (internalised) facets. The left side of the prism is the sender (the brand) and the right side is the receiver (the customer) (textbook).

**Class example:** The faculty introduced the brand prism as the topic of the day and referred to David Aaker and "Cashman" (Kapferer) as two different schools of thought on branding. The class also discussed that when no suitable product exists in the market, a company must build a brand model and identify the characteristics it will follow, and that you should study seven to eight brands, including the role agencies play in building them.

**Business example:** Royal Enfield. Physique: the thump, the heavy metal body, the classic design. Personality: rugged and independent. Culture: Indian military and touring heritage. Relationship: a brotherhood of riders (rides like Rider Mania). Reflection: the free-spirited explorer. Self-image: "I am not a regular commuter, I am a rider."

**Remember:** Six facets: Physique, Personality, Culture, Relationship, Reflection, Self-image. A simple memory line: "PPCRRS".

**Exam tip:** When asked to apply the prism, draw the hexagon, label sender and receiver, and fill each facet with a concrete brand fact. Marks go to specific examples, not generic adjectives.

### Aaker's Brand Identity Model

**Definition:** David Aaker suggests that a brand can be seen from four perspectives: brand as product, brand as organisation, brand as person and brand as symbol. The identity has a core (timeless essence) and an extended part (textbook).

**Class example:** A group designing brand elements and a logo for a campaign said the client wanted the organisation represented as a person or a symbol to build its identity and image. This maps directly to Aaker's "brand as person" and "brand as symbol" perspectives.

**Business example:** Amul uses the Amul girl as a symbol and gives the brand a witty, topical personality through its hoardings. Air India used the Maharaja as a person-like symbol of hospitality for decades.

**Exam tip:** If a question asks how to build identity through a person or a symbol, name Aaker's four perspectives and say which one you are using.

## Brand mantra

**Definition:** A brand mantra is a short phrase, usually three to five words, that captures the brand's essence. Kevin Lane Keller breaks it into three parts: emotional modifier, descriptive modifier and brand function (textbook). It guides internal teams on what the brand should and should not do.

**Class example:** The faculty said emotional modifiers are critical for long-term brand survival because they keep the customer in a positive frame of mind about the product.

**Business example:** Nike's mantra is "Authentic Athletic Performance" (emotional modifier: authentic; descriptive modifier: athletic; brand function: performance). Disney's is "Fun Family Entertainment".

**Formula:** Brand mantra = Emotional modifier + Descriptive modifier + Brand function

### Emotional modifiers: positive appeals

**Definition:** The emotional modifier is the feeling the brand evokes in its audience. Positive appeals use happiness, excitement, fun, elegance or a sense of legacy.

**Class example:** Cadbury, Maggi and Lego were given as brands built on happiness and excitement. Elegance and legacy were named as other positive appeals. Disney was the main example of a brand where fun is the core emotional modifier.

**Business example:** Cadbury Dairy Milk's "Kuch Meetha Ho Jaaye" ties the brand to celebrations and shared happiness. Titan uses elegance and legacy in its watch and jewellery (Tanishq) communication.

**Remember:** Customers prefer to stay in a happy zone with a feel-good factor. Positive emotion builds long-term attachment.

### Emotional modifiers: negative appeals

**Definition:** Some brands position around negative emotions such as fear and anxiety to create awareness or push the customer to act.

**Class example:** LIC was named as a brand associated with fear and anxiety, since life insurance makes you think about risk to your family.

**Business example:** LIC's long-running communication around protecting the family after the earning member is gone ("Zindagi ke saath bhi, zindagi ke baad bhi") draws on worry about the family's future, then offers security as the answer.

**Exam tip:** A fear appeal works for low-interest, high-risk categories like insurance and health. Mention that it needs a clear solution, or the customer simply avoids the message.

### Case: heart health and cooking oil positioning

**Definition:** A shift from negative to positive positioning means the brand first creates concern about a problem, then moves to a hopeful, benefit-led message once awareness is built.

**Class example:** Certain oil brands first ran fear-based campaigns showing the family breadwinner shaken by the risk of the wrong oil, suggesting it could even lead to the end of the family. Later the positioning moved to heart protection and positive health outcomes, tied to World Heart Day. The faculty stressed that this move from negative to positive is essential because customers prefer a happy, feel-good zone.

**Business example:** Saffola (Marico) built its brand on heart health, ran heart-risk awareness messages and regularly runs World Heart Day activities such as heart health check-ups, while its communication also shows positive, healthy family life.

**Remember:** Fear gets attention. Positive emotion keeps the customer.

### Descriptive modifiers

**Definition:** The descriptive modifier clarifies the nature of the brand or the kind of experience it offers. It sets the boundaries of what the brand covers.

**Class example:** The faculty introduced descriptive modifiers as a separate element of the mantra, focused on the experience the brand gives.

**Business example:** In Disney's "Fun Family Entertainment", "family" is the descriptive modifier. It tells you the experience is meant for families, which is why Disney keeps its core brand clear of adult-only content.

## Consistency of the brand mantra

### Global consistency with local adaptation

**Definition:** The core brand identity and mantra stay the same in every market. Only the approach and execution are adapted to the local psyche.

**Class example:** The faculty said a brand must remain consistent as a global entity regardless of market, while the approach should be "Indianized" to fit the Indian context.

**Business example:** McDonald's keeps its global identity of quick, affordable, family-friendly food, but in India it dropped beef and pork and introduced the McAloo Tikki and McSpicy Paneer.

**Exam tip:** Use the phrase "think global, act local" (glocalisation) and give one global and one local element of the same brand.

### Mantra consistency across joint ventures

**Definition:** When a brand enters joint ventures, co-brands or sub-lines, every programme must follow the same mantra so the customer does not get confused.

**Class example:** A group presentation showed a brand positioned on authenticity and performance. The feedback was that casual interpretations of the mantra must be avoided, so that the main brand keeps its performance-oriented image. Consistency is especially critical in apparel, where popular brands strictly follow the mantra so that the product stays clear in many markets.

**Business example:** Nike's collaborations (for example with athletes and designers) still carry the "Authentic Athletic Performance" core. Puma's tie-ups with Indian cricketers like Virat Kohli (one8) stay within its sport-performance positioning.

**Remember:** One mantra, many programmes. If a JV dilutes the mantra, the parent brand pays the price.

### Positioning segments and reach

**Class example:** In a review of a brand sold on Myntra, the group discussed its earlier focus on parallel categories and the need to target specific segments to stay relevant globally. The faculty asked whether the strategy was only for Myntra customers or for a wider audience, and asked the group to clarify the brand's pricing formula.

**Business example:** Roadster, Myntra's private label, targets young, value-conscious buyers on one platform, while a brand like Levi's sells across its own stores, marketplaces and multi-brand outlets.

**Exam tip:** Always state the segment and the channel. A positioning statement without a clear target and price logic is incomplete.

## Positioning cases from group presentations

### Affordable innovation

**Definition:** Positioning that combines new technology and good design with a price that a mass, young audience can pay.

**Class example:** One group positioned its brand on three pillars: stylish, affordable and tech-driven. The promise was "affordable innovation" for a young and connected lifestyle.

**Business example:** Xiaomi (Redmi) entered India with feature-rich smartphones at low prices sold online. Boat built a young, stylish audio brand at affordable price points.

**Exam tip:** Name the pillars, the target and the promise separately. That makes the positioning easy for the examiner to follow.

### Outdoor sports brand: from product list to value

**Definition:** A value-led positioning tells the customer why the product matters to them, instead of just listing what is on the shelf.

**Class example:** The group's brand focused on comfort, design and availability across sports equipment, sports shoes and fitness gear. Targets were beginners, casual users, professional athletes, and families and children. The value proposition was affordable pricing, a wide range and durability for long-term use. Based on feedback, the faculty recommended positioning it as an affordable sports brand for beginners and enthusiasts. You should move from "we have running shoes" to communicating affordability. Accessibility matters because beginners do not have the same needs as professionals.

**Business example:** Decathlon in India uses own brands like Kalenji (running) and Quechua (hiking) to make sport affordable and accessible for beginners, with large stores where you can try products.

**Remember:** Features tell, value sells. Speak in customer benefits.

### Regional market expansion

**Class example:** One group's company was stronger in South India than in North India. The plan was to use the strong existing customer base to grow sales and feed leads into the sales team.

**Business example:** Saravana Bhavan and MTR started with South Indian strength and expanded nationally by relying on loyal regional customers. Haldiram's grew the other way, from a strong North Indian base.

## What the faculty stressed

- The brand prism and Aaker's model are two different ways of looking at brand identity. Know both.
- Emotional modifiers decide long-term survival of a brand. Keep the customer in a positive frame of mind.
- Negative appeals (fear, anxiety) can create awareness, but the brand must move to a positive, feel-good position, as the oil brands did around World Heart Day.
- The brand mantra must stay consistent across markets, joint ventures and sub-lines. Avoid casual interpretations of it.
- Stay globally consistent but adapt the approach to the local, Indian psyche.
- In positioning, communicate value (for example affordability) and not just a list of products. Be clear on segment, channel and pricing logic.

## Practice questions

**Q1.** Explain the three components of a brand mantra with an example.
**A:** A brand mantra has an emotional modifier (how the brand makes you feel), a descriptive modifier (the nature of the experience) and a brand function (what the brand does). Nike's "Authentic Athletic Performance" uses authentic, athletic and performance in that order. Disney's "Fun Family Entertainment" follows the same structure.

**Q2.** Why do brands that start with a fear appeal often shift to a positive appeal? Use the cooking oil case.
**A:** Fear gets attention and builds awareness of a risk, but customers prefer to stay in a happy, feel-good zone. Oil brands first showed the breadwinner shaken by heart risk from the wrong oil, then moved to heart protection and healthy living linked to World Heart Day. The positive message sustains the relationship over time.

**Q3.** Describe Kapferer's Brand Identity Prism and apply it to an Indian brand.
**A:** The prism has six facets: physique, personality, culture, relationship, reflection and self-image, split between sender and receiver and between external and internal facets. For Royal Enfield, the physique is its classic design and thump, the personality is rugged, the culture is military heritage, the relationship is a riders' community, the reflection is the free-spirited explorer and the self-image is "I am a true rider."

**Q4.** Why must a brand mantra stay consistent across joint ventures?
**A:** Every JV programme carries the parent brand's name, so a casual reading of the mantra confuses customers and weakens the core image. A performance-led brand that allows casual JV products dilutes its authenticity. Apparel brands are especially strict because customers judge the brand directly by the product they wear.

**Q5.** How can a global brand stay consistent and still adapt to India?
**A:** The core identity and mantra stay fixed, while products, communication and pricing are "Indianized" to fit the local psyche. McDonald's keeps its quick, family-friendly identity but sells the McAloo Tikki and avoids beef and pork in India. This gives global recognition with local relevance.

**Q6.** An outdoor sports brand says "we have running shoes, cycles and fitness gear." Rewrite its positioning.
**A:** Listing products does not tell the customer why to choose the brand. A better position is "the affordable sports brand that makes it easy for beginners and families to start any sport", supported by a wide range, durability and accessible pricing. This matches the faculty's feedback and mirrors Decathlon's approach in India.
