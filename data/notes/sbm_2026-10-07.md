Prof Barkathunissa continued the Customer-Based Brand Equity (CBBE) pyramid, using Royal Enfield and Amul to show how salience, recognition and recall, a distinct identity, feelings and community-led loyalty build a strong brand. Groups then built brand models for their own brands, choosing names by emotional dimension and mapping imagery, performance, judgements, feelings and resonance. The second half covered how to calculate brand equity as the gap between subjective (perceived) and objective (actual) scores, with a soap attribute exercise, a motorcycle scoring case (TVS, Hero Slender, Yamaha) and a blind test.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning | Class example |
|---|---|---|
| Brand salience | How easily and often a brand comes to mind in a buying or usage situation | Royal Enfield recognised instantly on the road |
| Brand recognition | Identifying a brand when you see or hear it | Amul's purple colour and cow imagery |
| Brand recall | Retrieving a brand from memory without any prompt | Strong recall valued over simple recognition |
| Brand depth (depth of awareness) | How easily the brand is recalled or recognised | Prompt to go beyond surface-level examples |
| Prominence and image points | How strongly the brand stands out in memory, plus the associations attached to it | Two foundational blocks of the brand profile |
| Brand resonance | The overlap between the brand and the customer; loyalty and community at the top of the pyramid | Royal Enfield Moto events |
| USP | The distinct quality that sets the brand apart | Convenience, compactness or premium positioning |
| Subjective attributes | Perceived, judgement-based qualities that are hard to measure objectively | Freshness or feel of a soap |
| Objective attributes | Observable, factual characteristics that can be measured repeatedly | Fuel efficiency, load carrying capacity, soap density |
| Brand equity (score method) | Subjective brand score minus objective performance score | Hero Slender subjective score of 95 |
| Blind test | Testing a product with the brand name hidden to check whether scores come from the product or the brand | Volunteers stepped out for the class blind test |

## The CBBE pyramid applied: Royal Enfield

**Definition:** Keller's CBBE pyramid says brand equity is built in steps: salience (who are you?), performance and imagery (what are you?), judgements and feelings (what do I think and feel about you?) and resonance (what about you and me?) (textbook).
**Class example:** The professor broke Royal Enfield's strength into three layers:
- **Salience/awareness:** riders recognise the brand immediately on the road.
- **Distinctive identity:** a distinct ride performance and the unique identity of its sound (the "thump").
- **Loyalty and community:** loyalty programmes and community events such as Moto events strengthen the bond between rider and brand.

**Business example:** Royal Enfield organises Rider Mania in Goa every year and the Himalayan Odyssey ride, which turn owners into a community that promotes the brand by word of mouth.
**Remember:** Salience is the base, identity sits in the middle, community and loyalty sit at the top.
**Exam tip:** When asked to apply CBBE, take one brand and map a clear example to each level of the pyramid, the way the class did with Royal Enfield.

## Brand salience and awareness

### Brand salience

**Definition:** Brand salience is the degree to which a brand is recognised and comes to mind in a purchase or usage situation. It is fundamentally linked to brand awareness.
**Class example:** A Royal Enfield on the road is recognised at once, often by its sound before you even see it.
**Business example:** Ask an Indian buyer for a glucose biscuit and Parle-G comes to mind first. That instant recall is salience.
**Remember:** Salience is about being thought of at the right moment, not only about being known.

### Recognition versus recall

**Definition:** Brand recognition is the ability to identify a brand when you see it. Brand recall is the ability to retrieve the brand from memory without a prompt, for example when you only know the product category or the need. They are two distinct memory mechanisms.
**Class example:** The class stressed recall value over simple recognition. Amul is recognised through its purple colour, the cow imagery and the gold foil inside the chocolate pack, a tactile cue. KitKat was compared for how its packaging and the snap (hardness) of the product create lasting memories.
**Business example:** The Amul girl hoardings have run since 1966 and keep Amul in daily recall, while the blue-and-white "Utterly Butterly Delicious" visual cues make it easy to recognise on a shelf.
**Remember:** Recognition matters at the shelf; recall matters when the customer decides before reaching the store.
**Exam tip:** Recall is the stronger form of awareness. Mention both and say which one matters more for the category you discuss.

### Drivers of prominence and recall

**Definition:** The class named growth, trust and influence as the main pillars of brand prominence. Strong emotional connections, often formed in childhood, drive long-term loyalty and recall.
**Class example:** Amul was used as the case. Groups also discussed whether a brand jingle resonates with customers and works as a tool for memory and association.
**Business example:** Nirma's "Washing powder Nirma" jingle and Amul's "Amul doodh peeta hai India" are remembered decades later, which keeps both brands top of mind.
**Remember:** Childhood memories and sensory cues (colour, sound, jingle, packaging feel) build recall that lasts.

### Brand depth

**Definition:** Brand depth (depth of awareness) is how easily and likely a customer recalls or recognises the brand. Breadth of awareness is the range of purchase and usage situations in which the brand comes to mind (textbook).
**Class example:** The professor asked the class to move beyond surface-level examples and define brand depth, looking at the deeper layers of how a consumer perceives a brand.
**Business example:** Fevicol has strong depth, since carpenters and households recall it instantly for adhesives. Amul has breadth too, coming to mind for milk, butter, cheese, ice cream and chocolate.
**Exam tip:** Always pair depth with breadth in an answer. Depth is ease of recall; breadth is the number of situations where the brand is recalled.

## Building the brand profile

### Prominence combined with image points

**Definition:** A strong brand profile needs two foundational elements together: prominence (how readily the brand stands out in memory) and image points (the associations or imagery attached to it). Neither works well alone.
**Class example:** The professor called prominence the first foundational block of the profile and said it must be combined with image points to be effective.
**Business example:** Titan is both prominent (top of mind for watches in India) and rich in imagery (gifting, trust, Tata heritage), so its recall carries meaning.
**Remember:** Prominence without imagery gives empty recall. Imagery without prominence means nobody thinks of you at the moment of purchase.

### Feature elements

**Definition:** Feature elements are the specific components and product attributes that make up the brand profile, close to the "performance" block of CBBE (textbook).
**Class example:** For Royal Enfield, the distinct ride performance and the sound are feature elements.
**Business example:** Maruti Suzuki's fuel efficiency and low service cost are feature elements that shape its profile.

### Usage situations

**Definition:** Usage situations are the contexts or scenarios in which the brand is bought or used. Linking a brand to many usage situations widens its breadth of awareness.
**Class example:** Discussed as an additional component that adds to the profile's usefulness, alongside feature elements.
**Business example:** Cadbury Dairy Milk tied itself to celebrations and "something sweet" moments with "Kuch Meetha Ho Jaaye", which made it a choice for festivals and family events beyond a casual snack.
**Exam tip:** A brand profile answer should cover four parts: prominence, image points, feature elements and usage situations.

## Group brand model exercise

### Brand analysis framework

**Definition:** To build a brand model for a chosen brand, you work through these blocks: recognition and recall, USP, imagery, performance (and the brand line, its core message), performance dimensions, judgements, feelings and resonance.
**Class example:** USPs discussed included convenience, compactness and premium positioning. One group set brand goals around premium pricing and energy and planned targeting industry-wise and specialisation-wise, with resonance and events as the main engagement drivers. Another group pitched Octane pens: smooth writing with extra space, priced at Rs 10 for affordability, sold in bulk to colleges and stationery stores. The brand model had to show a strong link to the personal aspect, that is, the customer's own life.
**Business example:** Cello pens built a mass brand on affordability and smooth writing ("Cello Gripper"), sold through stationery shops and bulk institutional purchase, which is close to the Octane pitch.
**Remember:** A brand model must connect functional blocks (performance, USP) with emotional blocks (feelings, resonance).

### Naming by emotional dimension

**Definition:** Choose a brand name based on the feeling or emotional dimension you want customers to link with the product.
**Class example:** "Mom's Care" was proposed as a name that evokes warmth and trust. Johnson & Johnson was cited for excitement and security. BlackBerry was named as a pioneer in security, and Samsung Knox was described as perceived more secure than Apple on mobile security.
**Business example:** Mamaearth's name signals gentle, safe care for mothers and babies, and the brand started with toxin-free baby products to match that promise.
**Exam tip:** First decide the feeling you want to own (safety, excitement, care), then pick the name. The class action point was exactly this order.

### Judgements and feelings

**Definition:** Brand judgements are the customer's opinions about the brand, such as quality, credibility and superiority. Brand feelings are the emotional responses it evokes, such as warmth, fun, excitement, security, social approval and self-respect (textbook).
**Class example:** One group mapped their brand as follows. Associations: success linked to confidence, power and attractiveness, with the idea that a successful woman deserves applause. Feelings: familiarity and the image of family and friends sitting together. Performance: modern yet family-oriented. Judgement: quality, trustworthiness and comfort. Resonance: togetherness and meeting family needs drive repeat purchase. Excitement was another feeling discussed.
**Business example:** Tanishq's ads link the brand to confident, successful women and to family moments like weddings, which builds both self-respect and warmth.
**Remember:** Judgements come from the head; feelings come from the heart. Both sit on the third level of the pyramid.

### Brand resonance

**Definition:** Brand resonance is the overlap between the brand and the customer. High resonance means a strong match between the brand's identity and the customer's perception, ending in loyalty.
**Class example:** Factors that build resonance: frequency, reputation, recall and performance dimensions. The final aim of building resonance is to keep the customer loyal.
**Business example:** Apple and Royal Enfield owners show behavioural loyalty, attachment, community and active engagement, the four parts of resonance (textbook).
**Exam tip:** Draw resonance as two overlapping circles (brand and customer). The bigger the overlap, the higher the resonance.

## Measuring brand equity

### Subjective and objective attributes

**Definition:** Objective attributes are observable, factual characteristics that can be measured multiple times and that the customer can directly experience, for example in a product test. Subjective attributes depend on personal perception and have no objective standard, so they need qualitative analysis.
**Class example:** For soap, the class listed scent and fragrance, density (how long the soap lasts after several showers), the process and base used in manufacturing, moisturising after a shower, and the feeling of freshness, which can be checked through user testing. Mysore soap was compared with soaps from other states, with a difference of 5 to 6 degrees noted. The class also found that some categories are hard to judge because expectations are subjective and lack a universal standard.
**Business example:** Mysore Sandal Soap (KSDL) uses pure sandalwood oil, an objective attribute, but buyers pay a premium for its heritage and fragrance, which are subjective.
**Remember:** Objective = measurable and repeatable. Subjective = perceived and needs qualitative judgement.

### Brand equity as the gap between perception and performance

**Definition:** Brand equity is the gap between how good customers think a brand is (subjective score) and how good it actually is on measured attributes (objective score). The gap shows the value the brand name adds beyond the product.
**Formula:** Brand equity = Subjective brand score − Objective performance score
**Class example:** Motorcycle case:
- Subjective scores: TVS 84, Hero Slender 95, Yamaha 79.
- Objective, fuel efficiency: Hero Slender 9, Yamaha 8, TVS 7.
- Objective, load carrying capacity: Yamaha 6.
- Pickup: two of the brands need to improve pickup compared with the third.

The professor noted that the method works only if you use the correct calculation approach.
**Business example:** Hero Splendor and Hero's commuter range enjoy perception built on decades of "fill it, shut it, forget it" reliability, so buyers often rate them higher than spec sheets alone justify.
**Remember:** A positive gap means the brand adds value. A negative gap means the brand is underrated compared with its product.
**Exam tip:** Convert subjective and objective scores to the same scale (for example, both out of 100) before subtracting. Show the working for each brand.

### Benchmarking in the age of AI

**Definition:** A benchmark is the standard against which you compare scores. The class asked whether traditional calculation methods are outdated in the AI era.
**Class example:** Two ways to set benchmarks: organisational benchmarking (internal company data and standards) and targeted experiments using AI and neuroscience (for example, measuring brain and body responses to the brand).
**Business example:** Neuromarketing firms use EEG and eye-tracking to test ads and packaging for FMCG companies, giving objective data on attention and emotion.

### Blind test

**Definition:** A blind test asks consumers to rate products with the brand hidden. Comparing blind scores with branded scores shows how much of the preference comes from the brand.
**Class example:** Volunteers stepped out for a blind test to check the authenticity of the scores. Crunchiness and aftertaste were picked as the key attributes, with crunchiness as the top priority.
**Business example:** The Pepsi Challenge showed many people preferred Pepsi in blind tests, yet Coca-Cola led in branded tests. The difference is Coca-Cola's brand equity.
**Exam tip:** Use the blind test to explain brand equity in one line: same product, different score once the name is shown.

## Drivers of brand value

### Sentimental, nostalgic and social value

**Definition:** Beyond function, brands draw value from sentimental value (personal emotional meaning), nostalgic value (links to the past and childhood) and social factors (belonging, status and approval of others).
**Class example:** The class discussed these drivers across multiple brands and noted that you must separate the company-level perspective (corporate image) from the specific image or portrait of the brand in the customer's mind (user imagery).
**Business example:** Parle-G and Nirma use nostalgia in their advertising. Royal Enfield gains social value from its rider community.
**Remember:** These drivers feed the "feelings" and "resonance" levels of the CBBE pyramid.

## What the faculty stressed

- Brand salience is fundamentally linked to brand awareness. Recognition and recall are different; recall carries more value.
- Royal Enfield works as a full CBBE case: salience, distinctive identity (ride performance and sound), then loyalty and community through Moto events.
- Go beyond surface-level examples and be able to define brand depth.
- Prominence must be combined with image points for a brand profile to work; add feature elements and usage situations.
- Pick the emotional dimension first, then the brand name.
- Resonance is the overlap of brand and customer, built through frequency, reputation, recall and performance, and it ends in loyalty.
- Brand equity = subjective score minus objective score, but only the correct calculation approach gives a meaningful result. Validate scores with a blind test.
- Separate objective attributes (measurable) from subjective ones (needing qualitative analysis).

## Practice questions

**Q1.** Distinguish between brand recognition and brand recall with examples.
**A:** Recognition is identifying a brand when you see it, such as spotting Amul by its colour and cow imagery on a shelf. Recall is retrieving the brand from memory with no prompt, such as naming Parle-G when asked for a glucose biscuit. Recall is the stronger form of awareness and matters most when customers decide before reaching the store.

**Q2.** Apply the CBBE pyramid to Royal Enfield.
**A:** At the base, Royal Enfield has strong salience as riders recognise it instantly on the road. In the middle, its distinct ride performance and unique sound give it a clear identity. At the top, loyalty programmes and community events such as Rider Mania build resonance and a lasting rider-brand bond.

**Q3.** Subjective scores are TVS 84, Hero Slender 95 and Yamaha 79. On fuel efficiency (out of 10), Hero Slender scores 9, Yamaha 8 and TVS 7. Calculate brand equity on this attribute and interpret it.
**A:** Convert objective scores to a 100 scale: Hero Slender 90, Yamaha 80, TVS 70. Brand equity = subjective minus objective, so Hero Slender +5, Yamaha −1 and TVS +14. TVS gains the most from its brand name relative to its fuel efficiency, while Yamaha's perception slightly trails its product performance.

**Q4.** Differentiate between objective and subjective attributes using the soap example.
**A:** Objective attributes are measurable and repeatable, such as soap density, which decides how many showers the bar lasts. Subjective attributes depend on perception, such as fragrance appeal or the feeling of freshness, and need qualitative analysis or user testing. Mysore Sandal Soap combines a measurable sandalwood content with a strong subjective heritage appeal.

**Q5.** Define brand resonance and list the factors that build it.
**A:** Brand resonance is the overlap between the brand and the customer, where the brand's identity matches the customer's perception. The class named frequency, reputation, recall and performance dimensions as the factors that build it. The end goal is customer loyalty, as seen in the Royal Enfield rider community.

**Q6.** How does a blind test help measure brand equity?
**A:** A blind test asks consumers to rate products with the brand hidden, so scores reflect only the product. Comparing these with branded scores shows how much preference the name adds. In the Pepsi Challenge, Pepsi won blind tests but Coca-Cola led in branded tests, which shows Coca-Cola's brand equity.
