This IMC Monday built the communication model from the ground up: how a message is encoded, carried and decoded, why it gets distorted, and what that means for advertising. Exercises on script direction, The telephone game and an instruction sheet made the point, followed by noise, repetition, roadblock buying, fields of experience, barriers, persuasion and the 1972 Usha ad used for CIA 1.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning | Class example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Encoding | Turning a thought into a message | Writing an ad headline |
| Decoding | The receiver interpreting the message | Reading the ad |
| Medium | What carries the message | Air carries sound; no sound in a vacuum |
| Noise | Anything that distorts or blocks the message | Fest rehearsals, thinking about lunch |
| Feedback | The receiver's later reaction, collected by the sender | Sales after a campaign |
| Response | The immediate reaction | People leaving a cinema |
| Field of experience | The background that shapes how each person reads a message | Snapchat versus Instagram overlap |
| Roadblock | Same ad on all major channels at the same time | Prime-time 7 to 9 pm |
| Persuasiveness | The sender's power to influence | Speaker skill |
| Persuasibility | How easily the receiver is influenced | Audience openness |

## Parts of a print ad

1. **Headline:** the hook.
2. **Illustration:** the visual.
3. **Copy:** the whole layout.
4. **Body copy:** the product text (the KFC ad had none).

**Class example:** KFC's "Succumb to the crumb" plays on "bread crumb". Cutting a word made it sharper, and the silent b rhymes. Clients want safety and fear being too clever; copywriters want freedom and trust the audience to fill gaps.
**Business example:** Fevicol's ads by Ogilvy, such as the overcrowded bus held together by Fevicol, use almost no words. The visual carries the whole message.
**Exam tip:** When analysing a print ad, name all four parts and say what each does.

## The communication model

**Definition:** Communication is a sender encoding a message, a medium carrying it, and a receiver decoding it. This builds on the Shannon and Weaver model (1949), which added noise as the thing that distorts the signal (textbook).
**Class example:** Air carries sound; there is no sound in a vacuum. Interpretation is decoding.
**Remember:** The law of communication: communication fails, except by chance.

### Hearing versus listening

**Definition:** Hearing is sound reaching the eardrum. Listening is active attention to meaning.

### Encoding versus encryption

**Definition:** Encoding is any conversion of a message into a form. Encryption is encoding for a chosen audience only, such as Morse code or binary.

### Intrapersonal communication

**Definition:** Communication where sender and receiver are the same person.
**Class example:** Thinking is talking to yourself; overthinking is over-talking to yourself. A soliloquy is a character thinking aloud. In dreaming the subconscious sends and the conscious receives; in praying, the reverse.

### Match your language to your audience

**Class example:** A US plumber asked whether hydrochloric acid clears drains. The Bureau of Standards replied in jargon, which he read as a yes. The final reply: "Don't use hydrochloric acid. It eats the hell out of pipes."
**Business example:** Zomato's push notifications and social posts use casual, meme-style language because its young audience decodes that tone instantly.

## Why messages get distorted

### Script direction

**Class example:** A three-panel soft drink billboard (hot man, drinks, relieved) was read right to left in the Gulf, so readers saw relief, then the drink, then distress. English readers scan in a Z-pattern.
**Remember:** Language is not script. English uses Roman script; Hindi, Sanskrit and Marathi use Devanagari; Punjabi uses Gurmukhi; Urdu reads right to left.
**Business example:** Global brands adapt ad layouts for Arabic markets for this reason. The billboard story is a widely told marketing anecdote, so use it as an illustration rather than a verified case.

### The telephone game

**Class example:** "The little boy carried a bright yellow umbrella to school on a rainy morning" came out as "Little boy carried a water bottle to school." Mass messages always get distorted.

### The instruction sheet

**Class example:** The first line said to read everything and do nothing until item 20. Of 42 students, only Saurav came close, and he still wrote his name and address.
**Key idea:** People skim. Put the most important instruction where it cannot be missed.

### The Mandela effect

**Definition:** Many people sharing the same false memory. The term was coined by Fiona Broome in 2009.
**Class example:** India has no national language and no official national game. It is "Maggi" with no "e", and "Thums Up" with no "b".

## Noise, repetition and media buying

### Response versus feedback

**Definition:** Response is the immediate reaction; feedback comes later and is collected by the sender.
**Class example:** A reporter interviewing people leaving a cinema captures response.

### Noise

**Definition:** Noise is anything that distorts or blocks a message. Physical noise includes law students on break and fest rehearsals. Mental noise includes thinking about lunch or placements in class.
**Class example:** At an IIT Kanpur workshop the faculty learned that only about 10% of a class listens at any moment, so each point needs about 10 repetitions.

### Repetition and herd behaviour

**Class example:** If one sheep jumps a barbed-wire fence at one spot, the other 99 follow at the same spot. Each ad exposure is a sheep leaving a bit of wool on the fence, and some always sticks.
**Business example:** Cricket broadcasts repeat the same ads many times in one match. Brands such as Dream11 buy heavy IPL frequency because recall builds with repetition.
**Remember:** Advertising is iterative because audiences rarely take in a message the first time.

### Roadblock advertising

**Definition:** A roadblock places the same ad on all major channels in the same time slot, so switching channels shows the same ad.
**Class example:** Star Plus, Sony, Colors and Zee, 7 to 9 pm.
**Business example:** Brands launching big products in India, such as new smartphones, often buy roadblocks during prime time or across IPL broadcasts and streaming.

### TRP versus IMDb

**Definition:** TRP (television rating point), measured in India by BARC, shows how many people watch. An IMDb rating shows how highly viewers rate quality.
**Class example:** *Anupamaa* has high TRP and a low IMDb rating, and its audience is wider than people assume.
**Exam tip:** Media planners buy on reach (TRP), not on critics' ratings.

### Communication synergy

**Definition:** Using the same star or theme across a show and the ads in its breaks so each supports the other.
**Class example:** Bhuvan Bam in ads during his own series; Shah Rukh Khan ads during a Shah Rukh Khan film.
**Business example:** Bhuvan Bam's series *Taaza Khabar* streamed on Disney+ Hotstar in 2023 (the class said Prime), and brands featuring him gained from the overlap.

## Fields of experience, cohorts and barriers

**Definition:** Communication works in proportion to the overlap between sender's and receiver's fields of experience. This idea comes from Wilbur Schramm's model (textbook).
**Class example:** The faculty does not use Snapchat, so there is no overlap there, but both sides use Instagram.
**Definition:** A cohort shares experiences, not necessarily an age.
**Class example:** Partition, the Mandal agitation (1990), the JP movement (1970s), Anna Hazare (2011), 26/11 and COVID-19.

### Four barriers

1. **Physical:** noise, line static, a voice too soft or too loud.
2. **Semantic:** word meaning, pronunciation, generational slang (Gen X gets ignored in Boomer, Millennial and Gen Z talk).
3. **Cultural:** the West is direct; India, Japan and China are indirect.
4. **Psychological:** feeling overwhelmed, or an inferiority or superiority complex.

**Business example:** KFC's "finger-lickin' good" was reportedly translated in China as "eat your fingers off", a semantic barrier often quoted in marketing classes.

### Persuasion

**Definition:** Persuasiveness is the sender's power to influence. Persuasibility is how easily the receiver is influenced.
**Class example:** The faculty warned against motivational speakers and MLM schemes, which target high persuasibility.

## The Usha ad and CIA 1

**Class example:** The 1972 Usha sewing machine ad by Grant Advertising shows a mother and daughter, both educated and happy, with stereotypes typical of the time. The class compared it to Burger King's "women belong in the kitchen" ad.
**Business example:** Burger King UK tweeted "Women belong in the kitchen" on International Women's Day 2021 to promote a scholarship for women chefs. The joke drew heavy backlash, and the brand apologised and deleted the tweet.

## What the faculty stressed

- Communication fails except by chance, so repeat and simplify.
- Match language to the audience's field of experience.
- Know the four barriers with examples.
- Extroversion is a professional necessity; speak up for your 15 participation marks.

## Practice questions

**Q1.** Explain the communication model and the role of noise, with an advertising example.
**A:** A sender encodes a message, a medium carries it and a receiver decodes it. Noise, physical or mental, distorts the message along the way. Advertisers repeat messages, as in cricket broadcasts, because noise means most people miss the first exposure.

**Q2.** What is roadblock advertising and when would you use it?
**A:** A roadblock places the same ad on all major channels in the same slot, so viewers cannot escape it by switching. It suits big launches that need maximum reach in a short time, such as a prime-time slot across Star Plus, Sony, Colors and Zee.

**Q3.** List the four barriers to communication with an example of each.
**A:** Physical (a noisy classroom), semantic (slang or a poor translation), cultural (direct Western versus indirect Asian styles) and psychological (feeling overwhelmed by a senior person).

**Q4.** Differentiate TRP from an IMDb rating, and explain which a media planner uses.
**A:** TRP measures how many people watch; IMDb measures how viewers rate quality. Media planners buy on TRP because reach drives ad exposure. *Anupamaa* shows high TRP with a low IMDb rating.

**Q5.** What does the Burger King Women's Day tweet teach about communication?
**A:** The brand encoded a joke leading to a scholarship message, but many readers decoded only the sexist first line. Without shared context the message failed, and the brand had to apologise.

**Q6.** Explain field of experience with an example.
**A:** Communication works only where sender and receiver share background. A message on Snapchat reaches no one who does not use it, while Instagram gives overlap, so the brand should communicate there.
