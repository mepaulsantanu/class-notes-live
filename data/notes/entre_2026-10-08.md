This class was a hands-on session on customer discovery interviews for a student laundry service idea. You learned how to screen respondents against the target segment before talking, how to run the conversation as an interview to understand needs rather than as a sales pitch, and how to read customer engagement. The session ended with an evaluation of team interviews (Manu, Francis and Daniel), which pointed to weak trust-building and pitching too early.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning | Class example |
|---|---|---|
| Customer discovery interview | A conversation with a potential customer to learn about their problem and behaviour before selling | Interviewing students about their laundry needs |
| Target segment | The specific group of customers the venture is built for | Students, checked by name, age and student status |
| Screening | Checking that a respondent fits the target segment before the interview | Interview stops if the lead does not fit the demographic |
| Customer scenario | The real-life context in which the customer faces the problem | Living in a hostel versus living at home |
| Problem validation | Testing whether customers really have the problem and care about solving it | Asking whether students need a one-day laundry service |
| Product demo | Showing the product to explain what it does | Demo shown to the interviewer, not used as a sales pitch |
| Premature pitching | Selling the product before understanding the customer's need | Teams started pitching too early in the conversation |
| Rapport (trust-building) | The comfort and trust that makes a customer open up | Lack of bonding observed in the evaluated interviews |
| Customer engagement | How much the customer talks, shares and shows interest | Short yes/no answers and low talk time signalled low interest |
| Digging phase | The probing part of the interview where you ask follow-up questions | Teams asked many questions while digging |

## Customer discovery interviews

### Purpose of the interview
**Definition:** A customer discovery interview is a structured conversation with a potential customer to learn about their problem, current behaviour and willingness to pay, before you build or sell. (textbook) This is the first step of Steve Blank's customer development model.
**Class example:** The team planned to approach students and frame the interaction as a survey or interview to understand their needs. A key question was whether students actually require a one-day laundry service.
**Business example:** UClean, the Delhi-based laundry franchise chain, grew by studying how urban households and students handle washing, ironing and turnaround time. Fast turnaround became part of its offer because customers said waiting several days was a pain point.
**Remember:** The goal is to learn, not to sell. You judge success by how much you learn about the customer's problem.
**Exam tip:** (textbook) Mention Rob Fitzpatrick's "The Mom Test": ask about the customer's life and past behaviour, not their opinion of your idea, because people are polite and will praise any idea.

### Interview versus sales pitch
**Definition:** An interview collects information from the customer. A pitch tries to persuade the customer to buy. Mixing the two too early biases the answers.
**Class example:** The class clarified that the product demonstration should be shown to the interviewer (the evaluator), and it should not be treated as a customer sales pitch.
**Business example:** Before scaling, Zomato's founders collected restaurant menus and studied how office workers chose where to eat (Foodiebay stage). They understood the search problem first and only later built paid listings for restaurants.
**Remember:** Talk less, listen more. The customer should do most of the talking.

## Screening and customer profiling

### Verifying the target demographic
**Definition:** Screening means confirming that a respondent belongs to your target segment before you spend time interviewing them. Data from the wrong people misleads you.
**Class example:** The team decided to first check whether a lead fits the target demographic (name, age and student status) before going further. If the person does not match the target segment, the interview does not proceed.
**Business example:** Survey and research firms in India, such as Kantar, use screener questions (age, city, occupation, product usage) at the start of every study and drop respondents who do not qualify.
**Remember:** Ten interviews with the right people beat fifty with random people.
**Exam tip:** When you describe your research method, state your screening criteria clearly. It shows the sample matches the segment.

### Understanding the customer's scenario
**Definition:** The customer scenario is the real situation in which the problem shows up. The same product can matter a lot in one scenario and very little in another.
**Class example:** The team had to find out whether each student lives in a hostel or at home. A hostel student with no washing machine has a stronger laundry problem than a student whose family handles washing at home.
**Business example:** Stanza Living and Zolo bundle laundry into their student and young-professional housing because residents away from home do not have family support or space for a washing machine.
**Remember:** Segment by situation, not just demographics. Two 20-year-old students can have very different needs.

## Reading engagement and evaluating interviews

### Customer engagement signals
**Definition:** Engagement signals show how interested the customer really is: how long they talk, how much detail they share, and whether they ask questions back.
**Class example:** Santanu Paul observed that the customer's share of talk and engagement time were very low, with mostly brief yes/no responses. The customer showed little interest in the offers, so the conversion potential was judged to be low.
**Business example:** (textbook) In startup research, strong signals include a customer describing workarounds they already use, asking when the product will launch, or offering to pre-pay. Dunzo's early growth came from WhatsApp users repeatedly asking for errands to be run, a clear signal of real demand.
**Remember:** Polite yes/no answers are not validation. Look for stories, detail and commitment.
**Exam tip:** If asked how to judge interview quality, mention talk-time ratio, depth of answers and any commitment (time, money or referral) from the customer.

### Evaluation of team interviews (Manu, Francis and Daniel)
**Definition:** Interview evaluation scores how well an interviewer built rapport, probed the problem, collected data and avoided bias.
**Class example:** Santanu Paul reported the scores for Manu, Francis and Daniel against set criteria, with a score of 7.5 mentioned. Their strengths: they asked many questions in the digging phase and collected the business data they needed. Their weaknesses:
- Customers were not very responsive.
- There was little trust or bonding in the conversation.
- One customer dominated while the other stayed silent.
- They started pitching too early and moved away from the customer's specific needs.
**Business example:** Amazon's "Working Backwards" approach starts every new product from the customer's problem and writes a press release and FAQ around that need before building. Pitching features first would go against this.
**Remember:** Build trust first, dig into the problem next, and show the product last, if at all.

### Trust-building in interviews
**Definition:** Trust-building (rapport) is creating comfort so the customer shares honest information. Without it, answers stay short and guarded.
**Class example:** The lack of trust and bonding was the main reason customers in the evaluated interviews gave little response.
**Business example:** Meesho trained its resellers to sell through friends and family on WhatsApp because buyers trusted people they already knew. Trust moved the conversation forward faster than any product pitch.
**Remember:** In group interviews, draw in the silent person directly by name so one voice does not dominate the data.
**Exam tip:** Name practical rapport steps: introduce yourself and the purpose, start with easy questions about their routine, listen actively, and summarise what they said back to them.

## What the faculty stressed

- Screen first: check name, age and student status, and stop if the person is outside the target segment.
- Understand the customer's scenario (hostel or home) before judging the need.
- Treat the conversation as an interview to learn needs, not a sales pitch. The demo is for the interviewer.
- Test the specific need, such as whether students want one-day laundry service.
- Short yes/no answers and low engagement mean low conversion potential.
- Build trust before digging, and do not pitch too early.
- Manage group interviews so one customer does not dominate.

## Practice questions

**Q1.** What is a customer discovery interview and how is it different from a sales pitch?
**A:** It is a conversation to understand a potential customer's problem, context and behaviour before you sell. A sales pitch tries to persuade the customer to buy. If you pitch too early, the customer reacts to your product instead of describing their real problem, so the data becomes biased.

**Q2.** Why should you screen respondents before an interview? Give the criteria used in class.
**A:** Screening makes sure you collect data only from people in your target segment, so your conclusions apply to the right customers. In class, the team checked name, age and student status. If the respondent did not match the segment, the interview did not continue.

**Q3.** For a one-day laundry service aimed at students, why does the customer's living scenario matter?
**A:** A student in a hostel usually has no washing machine and no family support, so the need for quick laundry is strong. A student living at home may have the work done by family and feel little need. Knowing the scenario helps you segment correctly and judge real demand.

**Q4.** List the signals that tell you a customer is not engaged in an interview.
**A:** Short yes/no answers, low talk time, little detail about their own experience and no interest in offers are all weak signals. In class, these signs led to the conclusion that conversion potential was low. Strong signals would be stories of current workarounds, follow-up questions or a willingness to commit.

**Q5.** Based on the class evaluation of Manu, Francis and Daniel, what went wrong and how would you fix it?
**A:** They asked many questions and gathered the business data, but there was little trust, one customer dominated, and they pitched too early. You would fix this by building rapport at the start, involving every participant by name, and holding back the product until the problem is fully understood.

**Q6.** Explain the "Mom Test" and how it applies to customer interviews. (textbook)
**A:** Rob Fitzpatrick's Mom Test says you should ask about the customer's life and past behaviour, not whether they like your idea, because people give polite but false praise. For the laundry idea, ask "How did you get your clothes washed last week?" instead of "Would you use our service?". Facts about past behaviour are far more reliable than opinions about the future.
