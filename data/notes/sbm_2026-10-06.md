Prof Barkathunissa reviewed branding basics and moved into brand equity: what it is, how it is measured, and how customer lifetime value, brand strength and brand valuation relate to it. The class built the Customer-Based Brand Equity (CBBE) pyramid, touched on the brand identity model, and used Tropicana, Savlon vs Dettol, Apple, Google and Tesla to explain salience, positioning, points of difference and emotional branding.

## Branding fundamentals

**Definition:** A brand is a name, term, sign, symbol or design that identifies a seller's offer and separates it from competitors (textbook). Branding is the process of giving the product that identity and meaning.
**Class example:** The class reviewed the top brands of 2026 and the top 10 successful Indian brands, looked at what drove their growth and recognition, and covered two branding models used to plan and build brand identity.
**Business example:** Tata, Amul and Asian Paints regularly rank among India's most valuable brands because of long histories of trust and wide reach.
**Exam tip:** Open brand answers with a definition, then name the model you are applying (CBBE or the identity model).

## Brand equity

### What brand equity is

**Definition:** Brand equity is the extra value a brand adds to a product, seen in what customers know, feel and are willing to pay because of the name.
**Class example:** The professor used newspaper subscriptions to show how a brand is perceived and valued. Readers stick with a familiar paper even when others cost the same or less.
**Business example:** The Times of India and Malayala Manorama keep loyal home subscribers for decades because readers trust the masthead.

### Customer lifetime value

**Definition:** CLV is the total value a customer brings over the full relationship. In class it was treated as a reflection of the long-term bond with the brand, built from loyalty and trust.
**Class example:**
- Duration of relationship: a customer who stays for 25 or 50 years.
- Loyalty and trust together make up CLV.
- Share of mind: mental prominence of the brand, linked to market share. A 24% share of mind is considered very good.
- Cost of customer acquisition: a key input when working out the value of a customer over time.
**Business example:** LIC and SBI hold customers across generations, which gives them very high lifetime value per customer.
**Formula:** CLV ≈ (average value per period × number of periods the customer stays) − customer acquisition cost (textbook, simplified).
**Remember:** Long relationship + low CAC = high CLV.

### Brand capabilities

**Definition:** The operational strengths that support brand value.
**Class example:** Supply chain and logistics, human resources (quality of people and resources given to them) and organisational agility.
**Business example:** Amul's cooperative supply chain gets milk from villages to shelves every day, which makes its brand promise of freshness believable.

### Brand strength vs brand valuation

**Definition:** Brand strength comes from the non-monetary elements of a brand. Brand valuation is the intrinsic worth of a brand (heard as "native worth") and covers both monetary and non-monetary dimensions.
**Class example:** Discussed as two separate ideas that together explain brand equity.
**Business example:** Interbrand and Kantar BrandZ publish yearly brand values in dollars, while strength scores track loyalty and awareness.
**Exam tip:** Strength = non-monetary. Valuation = monetary + non-monetary.

### Methods of measuring brand equity

**Definition:** The class named three approaches:
- A calculated method based on specific metrics.
- A price-based method, where equity shows up in the premium a brand can charge.
- A psychological method that brand managers use to judge brand performance and value.
**Class example:** Qualitative methods look at customer knowledge, with CBBE as the main framework.
**Business example:** Tata Salt sells at a premium over unbranded loose salt; that price gap is a simple price-based measure of its equity.
**Remember:** Brand value is driven by innovation and user-friendliness, and relative worth is judged on these attributes.

### Apple logo evolution

**Definition:** Brand identity elements change over time as a brand matures.
**Class example:** Apple started with a different visual identity before moving to its current logo.
**Business example:** Apple's first logo (1976) showed Isaac Newton under a tree. It was replaced by the rainbow apple in 1977 and later by the single-colour apple.

## The CBBE pyramid

**Definition:** Keller's Customer-Based Brand Equity model builds a brand in four steps, shown as a pyramid. The left side is the rational route and the right side is the emotional route (textbook).
**Class example:** The pyramid drawn in class:
- Bottom: salience (heard as "style"), the base of brand awareness.
- Middle blocks: performance (left) and imagery (right).
- Next level: judgements (left) and feelings (right) (textbook).
- Top: resonance.
- Vertical lines separate the rational and emotional sides.
**Business example:** Royal Enfield moves riders up the pyramid. It is instantly recognised (salience), delivers a distinct ride (performance), carries a rugged image (imagery), and owners join rides and clubs (resonance).
**Exam tip:** Always draw the pyramid with labels on both sides. Diagram questions on CBBE are common.

### Stages of brand development

**Definition:** Each level answers a customer question: Who are you? What are you? What about you? What about you and me? (textbook).
**Class example:** The first stage asks "Who are you?" and "What are you?". Segmentation is known in theory, but a brand manager has to apply it in practice to find the right fit.
**Business example:** boAt defined itself early as an affordable, stylish audio brand for young Indians, which set a clear identity.

### The wavelength analogy

**Definition:** When a brand's "wavelength" matches the customer's, their thinking is aligned and a strong bond forms.
**Class example:** The professor used frequency and wavelength from physics to explain brand-customer fit.
**Business example:** Fastrack built its "Move On" youth positioning to match the attitude of college students.

### Resonance and aspirational brands

**Definition:** Resonance is the deepest level of the bond. Brands that reach it are aspirational.
**Class example:** Google was cited as a top brand that has achieved resonance with users.
**Business example:** Apple users in India often stay loyal across devices, a sign of resonance.

### Salience and prominence

**Definition:** Prominence (salience) is the overall recognition and visibility a brand holds in the mind. It covers awareness, recall and selection at the point of decision.
**Class example:** The CBBE discussion covered awareness, decision and recall value, and prominence. Distinctiveness keeps a brand top of mind so customers recall it when they intend to buy.
**Business example:** Maggi is so salient that many Indians say "Maggi" to mean instant noodles.

## Tropicana case: salience and usage situations

### Owning orange juice

**Definition:** A brand with high salience is the one a customer thinks of first in its category.
**Class example:** Tropicana dominates the mental space for orange juice with the claim that it is 100% orange, straight from the fruit.
**Business example:** Tropicana is owned by PepsiCo in India and competes with Real (Dabur) and Paper Boat.

### Category perception and shift to function

**Definition:** Brands can reposition a category by changing the value customers associate with it.
**Class example:** In India, orange juice is seen as a breakfast element and a lifestyle product. Brands are now trying to stress functional value, such as protein levels, to change how customers see the juice.
**Business example:** Amul launched high-protein buttermilk and lassi to give everyday drinks a functional reason to buy.

### Purchase vs consumption consideration

**Definition:** A brand must come to mind both when the customer buys and when they consume.
**Class example:** Buying for yourself differs from buying for others, such as for guests. The professor compared it with buying chocolate for yourself versus as a gift. Mind map exercises help find when and why the brand should appear in the customer's mind.
**Business example:** Cadbury Celebrations targets gifting occasions, while Dairy Milk bars target self-treats.
**Exam tip:** Mention usage situations whenever you discuss salience. Breadth of salience means coming to mind in many situations (textbook).

## Brand identity and the founder

### Founder image

**Definition:** The founder's image can drive how people identify the organisation and add to salience.
**Class example:** Tesla uses its founder's image to create brand salience. The brand name is the starting point for what a brand stands for, with Apple as the benchmark.
**Business example:** Ratan Tata's reputation strengthened trust in the Tata brand, and Kiran Mazumdar-Shaw is closely linked to Biocon.

### The six-element brand identity model

**Definition:** Kapferer's Brand Identity Prism describes a brand through six elements: physique, personality, culture, relationship, reflection and self-image (textbook).
**Class example:** The professor compared the model to how a person's qualifications and personality define them. The elements named in class were personality, culture, reflection and image.
**Business example:** Royal Enfield's physique is the thump and retro design, its culture is freedom and touring, and its reflection is the rugged rider.
**Remember:** Prism elements: Physique, Personality, Culture, Relationship, Reflection, Self-image.

### Premium vs aspirational consumers

**Definition:** Truly rich consumers rarely need brands to signal status. Aspirational consumers use brands to look good or be seen a certain way.
**Class example:** Ads with high-status imagery mainly target aspirational buyers.
**Business example:** Entry-level Mercedes-Benz A-Class and BMW 2 Series models are pitched at aspirational buyers moving up into luxury.

## Emotional branding

### Feelings over features

**Definition:** Customers forget what a brand said or did but remember how it made them feel. Feelings often matter more than specifications.
**Class example:** Brand value comes from the emotional experience and how the customer is treated, more than from the purchase itself.
**Business example:** Titan's "Celebrate" and Tanishq's wedding ads sell feelings tied to life moments.

### Confidence-based marketing

**Definition:** Some campaigns aim to raise the customer's confidence instead of market share, revenue or profit, giving them a sense of power.
**Class example:** Brands that do not push the product but put service and promises first build reputation value and loyalty.
**Business example:** Dove's Real Beauty campaign focuses on women's self-esteem rather than product claims.

### Logic plus emotion

**Definition:** A brand must offer both logical and emotional elements.
**Class example:** Customers who seek creativity and emotion form stronger attachments. Those who rely only on logic struggle to form the same bond.
**Business example:** Maruti Suzuki talks fuel efficiency (logic) and also family journeys (emotion).

### Influence of young consumers

**Definition:** Some segments influence purchases without paying for them.
**Class example:** In energy drinks, young consumers may lack purchasing power but strongly influence the brand. Some demographics are more sophisticated than company profiles assume.
**Business example:** Children shape family choices for cereals and snacks, which is why Kellogg's and Haldiram's advertise to them.

### Coca-Cola India: lifestyle value

**Definition:** Positioning a product around the lifestyle and aspirations of the customer.
**Class example:** The campaign should educate Indian customers on how the product fits a better lifestyle and should build on their aspirational value.
**Business example:** Coca-Cola's "Thanda Matlab Coca-Cola" tied the brand to everyday Indian refreshment.

## Positioning

### Product category vs usage condition

**Definition:** Category is what the product is. Usage condition is when and why it is used.
**Class example:** Categories such as non-alcoholic beverages (heard as "non-alcoholic milk") or hot beverages. A thirsty customer wants to quench thirst, and the context decides whether they pick coffee or another drink.
**Business example:** Paper Boat positions its drinks around nostalgia and Indian flavours, and Bru coffee targets the morning start.

### Point of difference: Savlon vs Dettol

**Definition:** A point of difference is a benefit that sets a brand apart and gives it its own space in the customer's mind.
**Class example:** Savlon claims "no more burning", targeting the sting of antiseptic. Dettol stresses strength and confidence. A brand's perceived strength or "hardness" signals reliability.
**Business example:** Dettol has long positioned itself on germ protection trusted by doctors and mothers.
**Exam tip:** Name the POD clearly, then show the competitor it beats. Savlon vs Dettol is a ready-made pair.

## Practice questions

**Q1.** What is customer lifetime value and what affects it?
**A:** CLV is the total value a customer gives a brand over the whole relationship. It rises with duration (25 or 50 years), loyalty, trust and share of mind. It falls as the cost of acquiring the customer rises.

**Q2.** Distinguish between brand strength and brand valuation.
**A:** Brand strength comes from non-monetary elements such as awareness, trust and loyalty. Brand valuation is the intrinsic worth of the brand and includes both monetary and non-monetary dimensions. Strength is one input into valuation.

**Q3.** Draw and explain Keller's CBBE pyramid.
**A:** The base is salience, or awareness. Above it sit performance on the rational side and imagery on the emotional side, then judgements and feelings. Resonance at the top is the deep bond that aspirational brands like Google achieve.

**Q4.** How does Tropicana build salience, and why do usage situations matter?
**A:** Tropicana owns orange juice in customers' minds with its 100% orange, straight from the fruit claim. Usage situations matter because people choose differently when buying for themselves and for guests, just as with chocolate for self or as a gift. Mind maps help place the brand in each situation.

**Q5.** Explain point of difference with the Savlon and Dettol example.
**A:** A point of difference is a benefit that sets a brand apart from rivals. Savlon positions on "no more burning", solving the sting of antiseptic. Dettol positions on strength and confidence, so each owns a separate space in the customer's mind.

**Q6.** Why is emotional branding powerful? Give an example.
**A:** Customers remember how a brand made them feel long after they forget its claims. Brands that put service, promises and customer confidence first build reputation and loyalty. Dove's Real Beauty campaign raised women's confidence instead of selling features.
