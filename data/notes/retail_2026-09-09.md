This RTM class explained how a retailer creates value and how stores keep shelves full. It compared India's organised and unorganised retail, used a US supermarket video to explain inventory systems, walked through a Bangalore distribution workflow and ended with a 10-minute written reflection.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning | Class example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Organised retail | Registered chains with formal systems | About 15% of Indian retail |
| Unorganised retail | Kiranas, hawkers and small independent shops | About 85% of Indian retail |
| Stock-out | A product missing from the shelf when a shopper wants it | 30% out of stock makes shoppers switch |
| Automatic reorder | Software that reorders when stock hits a threshold | US supermarket system |
| Shrinkage | Stock lost to theft, damage, spoilage or data errors | Why stores still do manual counts |
| Breaking bulk | Selling small quantities out of large supplies | One packet of milk a day |
| Assortment | The range of products offered | Many products in one place |

## Indian retail structure

**Definition:** Unorganised retail is kiranas and independent shops; organised retail is registered chains with formal systems.
**Class example:** Unorganised retail holds about 85% of Indian retail and organised retail about 15%. Big brands struggle to match the reach of the unorganised sector.
**Business example:** Reliance's JioMart works with neighbourhood kirana stores instead of trying to replace them, because kiranas already have the reach and customer trust.
**Remember:** A good product alone does not sell. You need promotion, service and activity around it.

## How a supermarket keeps shelves full

**Class example (video):**
- A typical US supermarket carries about 30,000 products and keeps them in stock about 92% of the time.
- The top four chains control about 45% of the market.
- If about 30% of what shoppers want is missing, they switch stores. Availability drives loyalty.

**Definition:** Automatic reorder is software that tracks items in and out and places an order when stock falls to a set level.
**Definition:** Shrinkage is stock lost to theft, damage, spoilage or data errors, which makes the system's count drift from what is on the shelf.
**Class example:** Stores do manual counts every one or two periods. The counts show true profit and recalibrate the software.
**Business example:** Walmart built its Retail Link system so suppliers could see store-level sales and restock faster. Shared data cuts stock-outs.
**Exam tip:** Link three ideas in one answer: stock-outs hurt loyalty, automatic reorder prevents them, and manual counts correct shrinkage.

## The value a retailer adds

1. **Proximity:** the shop is two minutes from home.
2. **Breaking bulk:** you buy one packet of milk a day, not four kilos at once.
3. **Holding inventory:** the retailer buys in bulk and stores it until you need it.
4. **Assortment:** many products in one place.

**Business example:** CavinKare sold Chik shampoo in ₹1 sachets from the 1980s, and the sachet became the standard way to sell shampoo in rural India. That is breaking bulk at scale.
**Business example:** Amul and Mother Dairy sell milk in 500 ml pouches so families can buy fresh milk daily.
**Exam tip:** Name all four functions and give one Indian example for each. This is a common short-answer question.

## Distribution workflow: Bangalore example

1. The city is split into North, East, Central and South zones.
2. Orders from agents arrive by about 6 pm.
3. Managers sort by zone overnight for next-day delivery, working around shop closing times.
4. Distributors start around 5 am.

**Class example:** Bread lasts 5 days to 2 weeks, so turnover must be fast, and case packing protects it in transit.
**Business example:** Britannia and Modern bread run early-morning delivery routes to kiranas so bread reaches shelves before the first customers.
**Remember:** Perishables drive urgency. Tracking all this is a real challenge for accounting and information systems.

## Pricing and location decisions

**Class example:** Opening in premium malls positions a brand as premium. Mall owners often block unknown brands, which acts as a barrier to entry.
**Class example:** Do not pick a price at random. Run a pilot study first. Heavy investment and a good product can still give zero profit.

**Definition:** Everyday low pricing (EDLP) keeps prices steadily low instead of relying on frequent sales.
**Business example:** DMart (Avenue Supermarts) follows EDLP. It owns most of its stores, keeps costs low and passes savings on as consistently low prices. Walmart built its global business on the same idea.

**Class example:** Multi-brand stores let a customer compare in one visit instead of going brand to brand. Bata came up as a contrast.

## The store manager's three jobs

1. Keep supply flowing.
2. Earn more from each customer.
3. Coordinate the team.

## Practice questions

**Q1.** Explain the four ways a retailer adds value, with an Indian example for each.
**A:** Proximity (the neighbourhood kirana), breaking bulk (₹1 shampoo sachets), holding inventory (a kirana stocking festival goods in advance) and assortment (DMart carrying groceries and household goods under one roof).

**Q2.** Why do supermarkets still do manual stock counts if they have automatic reorder systems?
**A:** Theft, damage, spoilage and data errors make the software count drift from the real shelf. Manual counts reveal true stock and profit, and recalibrate the system so future reorders are right.

**Q3.** What is EDLP? How does DMart use it?
**A:** Everyday low pricing keeps prices steadily low instead of running frequent sales. DMart keeps costs low by owning stores and running a tight range, then passes the savings on as consistently low prices.

**Q4.** How do stock-outs affect customer loyalty?
**A:** When too many wanted items are missing (about 30% in the class video), shoppers switch stores. Availability is a basic promise, and breaking it pushes customers to competitors.

**Q5.** Describe the distribution workflow for perishable goods in a city.
**A:** Orders arrive by evening, managers sort them by zone overnight, and distributors deliver from around 5 am. Short shelf life, as with bread, forces fast turnover and careful packing.

**Q6.** Why might a premium mall act as a barrier to entry?
**A:** Mall owners prefer known brands that draw footfall, so they often refuse unknown ones. A new brand may find it hard to get space there even with a good product.
