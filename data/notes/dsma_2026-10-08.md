## Purchase and revenue data: validating the metrics

### Reviewing the daily data
**Definition:** Metric validation means checking whether the data you have is complete and suitable for the analysis you want to do, before you start calculating.
**Class example:** The class reviewed the daily data section for August and September. It contained checkouts, purchase counts and revenue values, and the question was whether these metrics are enough to identify where users drop out and how well the site converts.
**Business example:** An Indian D2C brand such as boAt or Mamaearth tracks daily sessions, checkouts, orders and revenue in Google Analytics 4 or Shopify reports. Analysts first confirm that the numbers line up (for example, orders never exceed checkouts) before using them in a funnel report.
**Remember:** Each funnel stage should have fewer users than the stage before it. If a later stage shows a higher number, check the data before you calculate anything.
**Exam tip:** In any analytics answer, state your data period (here, August and September) and list the metrics you used. It shows the examiner your analysis is grounded.

### Session as the base metric
**Definition:** A session is a single visit to the website. In class it was confirmed that, for this project, a session represents one unique visitor.
**Class example:** Santanu Paul asked whether "sessions" meant total visits or unique visitors. The answer was that a session is taken as one unique visitor, so sessions became the base number for all rate calculations.
**Business example:** On Flipkart, one shopper who opens the app in the morning and again at night creates two sessions in Google Analytics, though they are one user. Reports separate "Sessions" from "Users" for this reason.
**Remember:** (textbook) In Google Analytics 4, sessions and users are different metrics. One user can create many sessions. For this project, follow the class convention and treat each session as one visitor, but mention the difference if a theory question asks.
**Exam tip:** Always say clearly what your denominator is. A rate is meaningless if the reader does not know whether it is per session or per user.

## The three-stage conversion funnel

### Funnel structure
**Definition:** A conversion funnel breaks the user journey into stages so you can see how many users move forward and where they leave. The class used three stages.
**Class example:** The faculty described three funnels in the user journey:
- **Entry:** users arriving on the platform.
- **Engagement:** how users interact with the site and discover specific products.
- **Conversion:** the final step that ends in a purchase.
**Business example:** On Nykaa, entry is a visit from an Instagram ad or Google search, engagement is browsing categories, reading reviews and adding to cart, and conversion is the completed payment. Nykaa watches each stage separately because a weak product page and a weak payment page need different fixes.
**Remember:** Entry problems point to traffic quality, engagement problems point to site content and product discovery, and conversion problems point to checkout and payment.
**Exam tip:** When asked to analyse a funnel, name each stage, give its numbers, calculate drop-off between stages, and then suggest one action per weak stage.

### Checkout and payment gateway drop-off
**Definition:** Checkout drop-off is the loss of users between starting checkout and completing the purchase. A large share of it often happens at the payment gateway.
**Class example:** The class looked at checkout conversion and the drop-off at the payment gateway as the last leak in the funnel before purchase.
**Business example:** Indian sites lose many orders to failed UPI or card payments and OTP timeouts. Razorpay and PhonePe promote features such as saved cards, UPI intent flows and one-click checkout (Razorpay Magic Checkout) to reduce this drop-off for merchants.
**Remember:** Checkout to purchase is the stage closest to revenue, so even a small improvement here adds revenue directly.

## Funnel calculations

### Conversion rate
**Definition:** Conversion rate is the percentage of sessions that end in a purchase.
**Formula:** Conversion rate (%) = (Number of purchases / Number of sessions) x 100
**Class example:** Santanu Paul asked what baseline to use for conversion rate. The faculty clarified that you divide purchases by the session number and multiply by 100. The current conversion rate for the site in the project came to **5.9 percent**.
**Business example:** If Nykaa records 2,00,000 sessions in a day and 4,000 orders, its conversion rate is (4,000 / 2,00,000) x 100 = 2 percent. Indian e-commerce sites usually report conversion in the 1 to 3 percent range (textbook), so 5.9 percent would be strong for a typical retail site.
**Remember:** Purchases go on top, sessions go at the bottom. Do not use checkouts as the denominator for overall conversion rate.
**Exam tip:** Show the formula, substitute the numbers, and write the final value to three decimal places, as agreed in class.

### Drop-off rate
**Definition:** Drop-off rate is the percentage of users lost between one stage of the funnel and the next.
**Formula:** Drop-off rate (%) = ((Users at earlier stage − Users at next stage) / Number of sessions) x 100
**Class example:** Santanu Paul explained the method for the first phase: subtract the next stage's number from the earlier stage's number, divide by the number of sessions, and multiply by 100. The drop-off for the first phase came to 30.315 percent.
**Business example:** If Myntra has 1,00,000 sessions and 40,000 of them view a product page, then 60,000 visitors left before reaching a product. The first phase drop-off is (60,000 / 1,00,000) x 100 = 60 percent. Myntra would then test better home page banners or search to reduce this.
**Remember:** (textbook) Many analysts divide by the users at the earlier stage instead of total sessions. That gives stage-to-stage drop-off. Dividing by sessions, as done in class, shows each phase's loss as a share of all visitors, so the phases can be compared on one base.
**Exam tip:** Calculate drop-off for every phase, then name the phase with the highest drop-off as the main problem area and suggest a fix for it.

### Rounding standard
**Definition:** A rounding standard is a fixed rule for how many decimal places you report, so that all figures are consistent.
**Class example:** The class decided that all numerical calculations will be rounded off to three decimal places, which is why the first phase drop-off was reported as 30.315.
**Business example:** Swiggy and Zomato investor reports present conversion and order metrics in a consistent decimal format across quarters so that small changes can be compared.
**Remember:** Round only the final answer. If you round middle steps, the final figure can shift.

## GA4 reporting

### Revenue tracking and data download
**Definition:** GA4 records revenue against purchases and lets you export the underlying report data for your own analysis.
**Class example:** The class confirmed that revenue tracking is available in the GA4 property and that the underlying data can be downloaded for calculations in Excel.
**Business example:** A Shopify-based Indian brand such as The Souled Store can link GA4 e-commerce events so each order's value appears in GA4, then export monthly revenue by channel to Excel or Google Sheets.
**Exam tip:** If a question asks how you would verify a funnel figure, say you would export the GA4 report and recalculate it yourself.

### Channel analysis using sessions
**Definition:** Channel analysis groups sessions by the source that brought users in (for example organic search, direct, paid search, social) so you can compare how each source performs.
**Class example:** The required data sits in Tab 3 of the GA4 dashboard. You filter it to show only four channels, chosen on the basis of session data, and present the KPI charts for those channels. Screenshots of the GA4 charts were chosen over manually retyping the same numbers into a table.
**Business example:** Lenskart compares sessions, conversion rate and revenue from Google paid search, Instagram ads, organic search and direct traffic. If one channel brings many sessions but few purchases, the budget for that channel is reviewed.
**Remember:** Pick channels by session volume first, then compare their conversion rate and revenue. A channel with high traffic and low conversion is a different problem from a channel with low traffic and high conversion.
**Exam tip:** Always pair a traffic metric (sessions) with an outcome metric (conversion rate or revenue) when you compare channels.

### Competition analysis
**Definition:** Competition analysis compares your site's traffic, channels and offer against rival sites to judge relative performance.
**Class example:** The Excel report has to answer a set of competition questions, which are in the file shared earlier, alongside the GA4 data.
**Business example:** Mamaearth tracks how much of its traffic comes from search and social compared with rivals such as Plum and WOW Skin Science, using tools like Similarweb, to decide where to spend on ads.

- Use sessions as the base for both conversion rate and drop-off rate in this project.
- Conversion rate = purchases / sessions x 100. The project site's rate was 5.9 percent.
- Drop-off for each phase = (earlier stage − next stage) / sessions x 100.

## Practice questions

**Q1.** Define a session and explain how it differs from a user.
**A:** A session is one visit to a website. In class, a session was treated as one unique visitor for the project. In Google Analytics 4, however, one user can create many sessions, so the two metrics are reported separately. Always state which one you are using as your base.

**Q2.** A website has 50,000 sessions and 1,200 purchases in September. Calculate the conversion rate.
**A:** Conversion rate = (1,200 / 50,000) x 100 = 2.400 percent. Purchases are the numerator and sessions are the denominator, as clarified in class. The answer is rounded to three decimal places.

**Q3.** Explain the three-stage conversion funnel used in class.
**A:** Entry is the stage where users arrive on the platform. Engagement covers how they interact with the site and discover products. Conversion is the final step that ends in a purchase. Measuring each stage separately tells you whether the problem is traffic, site content or checkout.

**Q4.** A site has 20,000 sessions. 13,937 users reach the product page. Find the first phase drop-off.
**A:** Users lost = 20,000 − 13,937 = 6,063. Drop-off = (6,063 / 20,000) x 100 = 30.315 percent. This matches the class figure and shows close to a third of visitors leave before reaching a product.

**Q5.** How would you compare marketing channels in GA4, and why filter by sessions?
**A:** Open the channel report (Tab 3 in the class dashboard), filter to the top channels by sessions, and compare their KPIs such as conversion rate and revenue. Filtering by sessions keeps the focus on channels that bring meaningful traffic. You then judge each channel on what that traffic achieves, not on volume alone.

**Q6.** Why is checkout and payment gateway drop-off important to track?
**A:** It is the last stage before revenue, so every user lost here had already shown strong purchase intent. Failed payments, OTP issues or extra form fields often cause it. Fixing this stage, for example with one-click or UPI checkout, raises conversion rate without needing more traffic.
