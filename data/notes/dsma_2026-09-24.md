This D&SMA class traced how web analytics grew from static pages to full interaction tracking, and how companies use that data to retarget buyers and check decisions. It separated predictive from prescriptive analytics, warned against following models blindly, and ended with a pair exercise mapping a buyer's journey with SMART recommendations.

## How web analytics grew

**Definition:** Web analytics is collecting and studying data on how people use a website.
**Class example:** The web moved from static pages you could only read to sites that record every interaction. The class dated Web Analytics 1.0 to around 2010.
**Business example:** Early analytics read server log files in the 1990s. Google bought Urchin and launched Google Analytics free in 2005, which made tracking standard for most websites. GA4 replaced the older version in 2023.
**Class example:** The workflow: collect data in sheets, upload to a drive, build visuals, then interpret them for the company's need.

## KPIs depend on the role

**Class example:** A social media lead tracks different KPIs from other managers. For e-commerce, track how much customers pay and how often.
**Business example:** A social media lead might track reach, saves and profile visits, while an e-commerce head tracks average order value and repeat purchase rate.

## Tracking behaviour and retargeting

**Definition:** Retargeting shows ads to people who visited a site or app but did not buy.
**Class example:** Apple, Lenovo and Facebook track time on page, products viewed and filters used (such as laptop specs), then retarget you with ads to bring you back.
**Business example:** The Meta Pixel on a brand's website records which products you viewed, so Instagram can later show you that exact product. Google Ads remarketing works the same way across websites and YouTube.

### The funnel

1. Visit.
2. Click.
3. Add to cart.
4. Purchase.

**Key idea:** Your job is to move people from engagement to conversion, and to use data to check whether a management decision was right.

## Predictive versus prescriptive analytics

**Definition:** Predictive analytics uses past data to forecast what will happen. Prescriptive analytics recommends the actions needed to reach a target.
**Class example:** Prescriptive: what to do to hit 100,000 steps.
**Business example:** Netflix predicts what you will watch next (predictive). UPS's ORION system tells each driver the best route to take (prescriptive), and UPS reports it saves about 100 million miles a year.
**Exam tip:** Predictive answers "what will happen?"; prescriptive answers "what should we do?". Give one company example for each.

## Never follow a model blindly

**Class example:** A manager should test the model and the data first, and know who is accountable if it is wrong.
**Business example:** Zillow shut its home-buying business, Zillow Offers, in 2021 after its pricing algorithm overpaid for thousands of houses. The company took losses of over $500 million and cut about a quarter of its staff.
**Remember:** A model is a tool, not a decision-maker. Someone must own the decision.

## Case: "the model says increase ads"

1. Define the outcome KPI and the promised ROI before acting.
2. Past performance is not a KPI for the future.
3. Engagement and interactions are not outcomes. Conversion is the only final outcome.
4. Start with a specific goal before opening a report, so you do not waste time on irrelevant numbers.
5. Win stakeholders with evidence and give specific answers.

**Formula:** ROI = (revenue from the campaign − cost of the campaign) ÷ cost of the campaign × 100 (textbook).
**Business example:** A campaign costing ₹2 lakh that brings ₹5 lakh in sales gives ROI = (5 − 2) ÷ 2 × 100 = 150%.

## Pair exercise: customer journey map

**Definition:** A customer journey map shows each stage a buyer goes through, with their thoughts, pain points and the channels they use.
**Class example:** One partner interviews the other as a buyer, asking about the person, not the product. Products offered: a home loan, an EV (a Mahindra EV at ₹20 to 25 lakh), an online MBA, a 3-day hotel package, or an OTT subscription.

### Steps

1. Build a buyer persona (example: a Bengaluru professional, with age, motives and behaviour).
2. Look at the financial side: what is the risk of lending or selling to this buyer (job security, employment gaps, a past layoff)?
3. Look at the marketing side: which channels lead to discovery, and what drives the decision?
4. Fill a findings table: pain points and confusion, turned into lessons for the company.
5. Output the buyer's risk level, the percentage chance they buy, how to track them and how to convince them, as an infographic plus a decision table.
6. Write at least 5 SMART recommendations per EV category, each tied to a KPI.

**Definition:** SMART goals are Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound. George Doran introduced the idea in 1981.
**Business example:** "Raise test-drive bookings from Instagram ads by 20% in 60 days" is SMART; "improve social media" is not.
**Business example:** Tata Motors led India's electric car market for several years with around 70% share in 2023, helped by the Nexon EV and a strong charging-partner network. Your pair leaned towards Tata on past client feedback.
**Remember:** You may write recommendations yourself or with AI; both are accepted.

- Recommendations must be SMART and tied to a KPI.

## Practice questions

**Q1.** Differentiate predictive from prescriptive analytics with examples.
**A:** Predictive analytics forecasts what will happen, as Netflix predicts what you will watch. Prescriptive analytics recommends what to do, as UPS's ORION tells drivers the best route, saving about 100 million miles a year.

**Q2.** How does retargeting work? Give an example.
**A:** A tracking tool such as the Meta Pixel records products a visitor viewed. The brand then shows ads for those products on Instagram or Facebook to bring the visitor back to buy.

**Q3.** Why should managers not follow an analytics model blindly? Use Zillow.
**A:** Models can be wrong, and someone must own the decision. Zillow's pricing algorithm overpaid for homes, leading to over $500 million in losses and the closure of Zillow Offers in 2021.

**Q4.** A campaign cost ₹1.5 lakh and generated ₹4.5 lakh in sales. Calculate the ROI.
**A:** ROI = (4.5 − 1.5) ÷ 1.5 × 100 = 200%. The campaign returned twice its cost in profit before other expenses.

**Q5.** Rewrite "improve our EV marketing" as a SMART recommendation.
**A:** "Increase EV test-drive bookings from Instagram ads by 20% within 60 days, measured through booking form submissions." It is specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound.

**Q6.** What are the steps in building a customer journey map?
**A:** Build a buyer persona, assess the financial risk, trace the marketing channels and decision drivers, list pain points in a findings table, estimate risk and purchase probability, and end with SMART recommendations tied to KPIs.
