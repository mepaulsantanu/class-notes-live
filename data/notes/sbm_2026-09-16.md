This SBM class built out brand identity: the outer image versus the inner identity, the layers of Aaker's model, brand essence, and each brand element from name to mascot. Student presentations on Shaktiman, Forest Essentials, Rolls-Royce and the Coca-Cola bottle showed the elements at work, and groups discussed criteria for comparing brands across stores.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning | Class example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Brand image | How customers see the brand from outside | Outward expression |
| Brand identity | What the brand stands for from inside | "The heart and soul" |
| Core identity | The central, timeless values of a brand | Tata: family, trust |
| Extended identity | Elements around the core that add detail | Logo, tagline, personality |
| Brand essence | One idea that captures the brand's soul | "The concentrated soul" |
| Brand elements | Name, logo, tagline, mascot, packaging, sensory cues | Nike swoosh and "Just Do It" |
| Mascot | A character that represents the brand | Amul girl, ZooZoos |
| Sensory branding | Using smell, sound, touch or taste to build memory | Forest Essentials |

## Brand image versus brand identity

**Definition:** Brand identity is what the company wants the brand to stand for, built from inside. Brand image is how customers actually see it.
**Class example:** Two sides: the outward image a brand creates, and the inner identity, "the heart and soul".
**Business example:** Patanjali built an identity around Ayurveda and swadeshi. Its image rose fast, then took hits after quality disputes and misleading-ad cases, showing that image can drift from identity.
**Exam tip:** Identity is sent; image is received. The gap between them is what brand managers work to close.

## Aaker's brand identity model

**Definition:** David Aaker's model (from *Building Strong Brands*, 1996) puts brand essence at the centre, core identity around it and extended identity on the outside.
**Class example:** The session described three layers: outward expression, internal identity, and at the centre the core characteristics. It labelled the centre "extended identity". In Aaker's model the centre is the core identity and the extended identity surrounds it, so follow the textbook in exams.
**Class example:** A brand is performance plus core identity plus appearance. Tata shows that core identity (family, trust) matters more than looks and makes the image easy to remember.
**Business example:** Volvo's core identity has been safety for decades. Volvo engineer Nils Bohlin invented the three-point seatbelt in 1959, and the company kept the patent open so every carmaker could use it.

### Brand essence

**Definition:** Brand essence is a single idea that captures the brand's soul.
**Class example:** "The concentrated soul." Class example traits were Goodness and Adventure.
**Business example:** Nike's essence is often summed up as "authentic athletic performance". BMW's is "Sheer Driving Pleasure".
**Remember:** Core values come first. Campaigns work only when founders believe in them. Parts of a brand can change, but not the core.

## Brand elements

1. **Name:** the first identifier.
2. **Logo:** the visual mark (Apple's apple, Nike's swoosh).
3. **Tagline:** a short phrase carrying the promise ("I'm Lovin' It").
4. **Mascot:** an animal or fictional character.
5. **Packaging:** shape and design.
6. **Sensory cues:** smell, sound and touch.

**Definition:** Keller's six criteria for choosing brand elements are memorable, meaningful, likeable, transferable, adaptable and protectable (textbook).
**Class example:** Apple (logo, the Jobs story, "creating") and Nike ("Just Do It"; Nike is the Greek goddess of victory).
**Class example:** A shampoo that gives more in a small pack and calls itself "revolutionary" creates a new category.

### Mascots and their risks

**Class example:** The "It" films hurt McDonald's clown image. Celebrities carry similar risk. Vodafone's white-suited characters came up as an example.
**Business example:** The white-suited characters are Vodafone's ZooZoos, launched during IPL 2009 by Ogilvy. They were cheap to produce, since actors in costumes, yet became hugely popular.
**Business example:** McDonald's cut back Ronald McDonald's appearances in 2016 during a wave of creepy-clown sightings in the US, ahead of the 2017 *It* film.
**Business example:** The Amul girl, created in 1966, has never aged, never had a scandal and never asked for a fee. That is the safety a mascot offers over a celebrity.
**Exam tip:** Mascot versus celebrity is a common question. Mascots are controllable and permanent; celebrities bring instant attention but also personal risk.

### Competitive positioning through elements

**Class example:** Burger King's jab: "Why buy from a clown when you can buy from a king?"
**Business example:** Burger King's "Whopper Detour" (2018) sold Whoppers for one cent to people who ordered through its app while standing near a McDonald's. Challenger brands use the leader's own presence against it.

## Student presentations

**Class example:** Shaktiman: power and strength, influencers for younger buyers, and a small biscuit product.
**Class example:** Forest Essentials: sensory marketing with jasmine and rose, luxury unboxing, and loyalty through experience.
**Business example:** Forest Essentials sells Ayurvedic skincare in heritage-style packaging, and Estée Lauder took a stake in it in 2008. Its stores are designed around fragrance and touch.
**Class example:** Rolls-Royce design and the Coca-Cola bottle shape.
**Business example:** The Coca-Cola contour bottle, designed in 1915, was trademarked so the shape itself identifies the brand, even in the dark.
**Business example:** Air India's Maharaja mascot, created in 1946, stayed relevant for decades. When Tata rebranded Air India in 2023 with a new logo, the Maharaja was kept for select uses.

## Comparing brands across stores (CIA group work)

**Class example:** MRP was dropped as a criterion because it is the same everywhere. Criteria chosen: offers and discounts (including card offers), customer engagement and satisfaction, and visual merchandising.
**Class example:** For a phone like the iPhone, customers care more about which cards give EMI than the store discount. Authenticity and after-sales matter: Reliance feels safe on authenticity, but some repairs still need an Apple store.

## What the faculty stressed

- The core identity never changes; the rest can.
- Every brand element should carry the brand promise.
- Mascots are safer than celebrities.

## Practice questions

**Q1.** Differentiate brand identity from brand image.
**A:** Brand identity is what the company wants the brand to stand for, built from inside. Brand image is how customers actually see it. Brand managers work to close the gap, as Patanjali learned when quality disputes hurt its image.

**Q2.** Explain Aaker's brand identity model with an example.
**A:** Aaker places brand essence at the centre, core identity around it and extended identity on the outside. Volvo's core identity is safety, shown by its seatbelt invention, while its logo and design sit in the extended identity.

**Q3.** List Keller's six criteria for brand elements.
**A:** Memorable, meaningful, likeable, transferable, adaptable and protectable. The first three build brand equity; the last three protect it.

**Q4.** Compare mascots and celebrity endorsers as brand elements.
**A:** Mascots are controllable, never age and carry no scandal risk, like the Amul girl since 1966. Celebrities give instant attention but bring personal risk and high fees. Even mascots can suffer from outside events, as McDonald's clown did in 2016.

**Q5.** How does Forest Essentials use sensory branding?
**A:** It uses fragrances such as jasmine and rose, heritage-style packaging and a luxury unboxing experience. The smell and feel of the stores and products build memory and loyalty beyond the products themselves.

**Q6.** How can packaging become a brand element? Use Coca-Cola.
**A:** Coca-Cola's contour bottle, designed in 1915, was trademarked so the shape identifies the brand on its own. A customer can recognise it by touch, which makes the packaging a lasting brand asset.
