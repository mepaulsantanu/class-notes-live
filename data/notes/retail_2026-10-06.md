## Store detailing and format

### Detailing

**Definition:** Detailing covers the physical and operational side of a store, including location, shop layout and furniture.
**Class example:** Floor area was named as a key variable for every store. Layout and range were reviewed as the base for applying the gravity model.
**Business example:** Zudio stores use simple fixtures, wide aisles and a compact range so the low-price format is clear from the moment you enter.

### Store format sets the boundaries

**Definition:** Store format is the foundational decision. Once you choose it, you build the target market around it. Stretching a store beyond its format makes it inefficient or too expensive.
**Class example:** A brand's main showroom and its factory outlet serve different customers. The factory outlet draws shoppers mainly through discounts all year round.
**Business example:** Raymond runs full-price Raymond Shops in malls and high streets, and separate factory outlets that sell older stock at a discount to price-sensitive buyers.
**Exam tip:** Write "format first, target market next". The class stressed this order.

## Retail strategy and competitive advantage

### Core elements of retail strategy

**Definition:** A retail strategy defines the retail mix, the target market and the route to a sustainable competitive advantage. It must also allow for the different structures of online and offline businesses.
**Class example:** Target markets differ by channel, as with the showroom vs factory outlet example.
**Business example:** Nykaa sells online to a wide audience and runs Nykaa Luxe stores offline for premium beauty shoppers.

### Sustainable competitive advantage

**Definition:** An advantage that rivals cannot easily copy. The class said six variables build it. The textbook (Levy and Weitz) lists customer loyalty, location, human resource management, distribution and information systems, unique merchandise, vendor relations and customer service (textbook).
**Class example:**
- Buying vs leasing property depends on company policy.
- Decision structures differ: a proprietor decides alone, while a committee decides in larger firms. In retail banking, South Indian Bank decides by target segment, such as corporate or retail.
- Entering a new market with a local businessman reduces risk and brings local knowledge, as with the example of a businessman partnering a local in China.
- High employee morale and good HR policies lead to better results. Happy employees are more motivated and perform better.
**Business example:** DMart owns most of its stores, which keeps rent costs low and lets it hold lower prices than rivals for years. Walmart entered India through a partnership with Bharti before buying Flipkart.
**Remember:** Location, people, partners and policy all feed the advantage.

## The role of location

### Location as the most critical factor

**Definition:** Location outweighs every other retail decision, because it is hard and costly to change.
**Class example:** Walmart and IKEA build their own infrastructure on city outskirts. A small momo shop can succeed in a narrow alley if the demand is there. D-Mart was taken up as a case of high traffic driven by clear target market demand.
**Business example:** IKEA's Bengaluru store at Nagasandra sits away from the city centre but next to a metro station, so it draws planned trips.
**Exam tip:** Start any location answer with "location is the most critical decision in retail" and then give scale-based examples.

### Market block vs out-of-city locations

**Definition:** A market block has heavy shopping foot traffic. An out-of-city site depends on customers making a special trip, so it needs different thinking.
**Class example:** Stores near a market (such as Block 4) gain traffic because people pass by while commuting, even if they are not visiting the main market.
**Business example:** Commercial Street and Brigade Road in Bengaluru are market blocks where fashion brands depend on walk-in shoppers.

### Access and the 10-minute goal

**Definition:** Good access to transport decides store success.
**Class example:** Aim for locations customers can reach within 10 minutes.
**Business example:** Quick commerce players like Blinkit and Zepto place dark stores so they can deliver within 10 minutes across a catchment.

### Changing shoppers

**Definition:** Digitalisation has made customers more willing to visit several stores and compare.
**Class example:** Women were named as a very powerful buyer segment who prefer visiting multiple stores before buying.
**Business example:** Mall clusters like Phoenix Marketcity let shoppers compare many apparel brands in one trip.

## Catchment area and demographics

**Definition:** Catchment analysis starts at the centre of a circle around the store and extends outward, usually 3 to 4 km, to study who lives there and what they can afford.
**Class example:**
- High-end areas are defined by the purchasing power of the target audience, as for Zara.
- Slum areas are excluded for such brands because people there cannot afford the products.
- Check whether the area is dominated by couples, singles or students.
- Match pack sizes to the population: students buy small quantities (1 to 2 kg), not bulk.
**Business example:** Zara opens in premium malls like UB City and Phoenix Marketcity, where the catchment has high spending power.
**Remember:** Catchment = distance + purchasing power + household type.

## Types of retail locations

**Definition:** Retail locations fall into types based on traffic, competition and how customers arrive.
**Class example:**
- Town locations: shopping centres and malls, such as Vega City Mall.
- Remote locations: no competing retail nearby.
- Downtown: dense foot traffic where many stores can be visited in minutes.
- Stand-alone stores on the outskirts: rely on destination shopping, not random foot traffic.
- Periodic markets: weekly or monthly markets such as Chor Bazaar.
- Drive-through shopping centres and service centres.
- Distance is perceived differently: 12 km is short in the US or Canada but long in India.
**Business example:** Decathlon often opens large stand-alone stores at the city edge, and shoppers drive to them on weekends.
**Exam tip:** Tie each location type to how the customer arrives: walking past, planned trip, or periodic visit.

### Power stores and one-stop shops

**Definition:** Power stores and discount stores sell large quantities at low prices, typically big buys of 15 to 20 units.
**Class example:** Some stores add integrated services, like a tailor attached to clothing sales, to become a one-stop shop.
**Business example:** Metro Cash and Carry (now owned by Reliance) sells in bulk to kirana owners and businesses.

## The STORIS framework

**Definition:** A framework for modern store strategy, as given in class:
- S: Social spaces
- T: Technology enabled stores
- O: Omnichannel strategies (heard as "home channel")
- R: Relevance and accurate offerings
- I: Influencer marketing, with micro-influencers preferred over TV celebrities
- S: Scalable personalisation
**Class example:** Micro-influencers were favoured because they connect more closely with a local audience than TV influencers.
**Business example:** Tata CLiQ and Nykaa let you order online and pick up or return in store, an omnichannel model. Nykaa works with thousands of micro-influencers for product reviews.
**Remember:** STORIS letters in order: Social, Tech, Omnichannel, Relevance, Influencers, Scalable personalisation.

## The gravity model

### Assumptions

**Definition:** The share of shoppers who use a shopping area depends on two things: the value or attraction of the area and the travel distance to it.
**Class example:** For daily needs, consumers prefer closer locations. Trade area analysis and the gravity model help place stores where the target market is concentrated.
**Business example:** Grocery formats like More and Ratnadeep sit inside residential neighbourhoods because daily needs shoppers will not travel far.

### Formula and variables

**Definition:** The formula gives the probability that a customer at a given origin travels to a particular shopping centre.
**Class example:**
- S: size of the shopping centre (floor area).
- G [T]: travel time or distance from the starting point.
- B [λ]: an exponent that captures how strongly travel time affects the consumer.
- Travel time matters more than raw distance: 9 km can take very different times by car or by other modes.
**Business example:** A 9 km trip in Bengaluru traffic can take 20 minutes or over an hour, so chains look at drive time rather than kilometres.
**Formula:** P_ij = (S_j / T_ij^λ) ÷ Σ (S_k / T_ik^λ), where P_ij is the probability that a customer at i shops at centre j (Huff model, textbook).

### Worked illustration

**Class example:** The problem set asks you to compare three locations A, B and C and pick the most preferable market, with the exponent set at 2. The numbers below are only for practice, not from class.
- A: 50,000 sq ft, 10 minutes → 50,000 / 10² = 500
- B: 30,000 sq ft, 5 minutes → 30,000 / 5² = 1,200
- C: 80,000 sq ft, 15 minutes → 80,000 / 15² = 355.6
- Total = 2,055.6. Probabilities: A = 24.3%, B = 58.4%, C = 17.3%.
**Remember:** With an exponent of 2, doubling travel time cuts attraction to one quarter. That is why the small but near location B wins.
**Exam tip:** Show each step: S ÷ T^λ for every centre, the total, then each share as a percentage.

## Practice questions

**Q1.** Why is store format called a foundational decision?
**A:** Format sets the boundaries of what a store can offer and whom it can serve. Once you choose a format, you build the target market around it. Trying to go beyond the format makes the store inefficient or too expensive, as the gap between a main showroom and a factory outlet shows.

**Q2.** What is a catchment area and what would you analyse in it?
**A:** A catchment area is the zone around a store, usually a 3 to 4 km circle, from which most customers come. You study purchasing power, as Zara does when it avoids low-income areas, and household type such as couples, singles or students. These decide the range and pack sizes, for example 1 to 2 kg packs for students.

**Q3.** Explain any four types of retail locations with examples.
**A:** Town locations include malls and shopping centres such as Vega City Mall. Downtown locations offer dense foot traffic where many stores are a few minutes apart. Stand-alone outskirts stores depend on destination shopping, as with IKEA, and periodic markets like Chor Bazaar run weekly or monthly.

**Q4.** Expand and explain the STORIS framework.
**A:** STORIS stands for Social spaces, Technology enabled stores, Omnichannel strategies, Relevance and accurate offerings, Influencer marketing and Scalable personalisation. It guides how a modern store stays useful to shoppers. The class stressed micro-influencers over TV celebrities because they build closer local trust.

**Q5.** State the assumptions and formula of the gravity model.
**A:** The model assumes that the share of shoppers visiting an area depends on its size or attraction and the travel time to it. The formula is P_ij = (S_j / T_ij^λ) ÷ Σ (S_k / T_ik^λ). S is the size of the centre, T is travel time and λ is the exponent showing how strongly travel time deters shoppers.

**Q6.** Three centres have S/T² values of 500, 1,200 and 355.6. Which is the most preferable and why?
**A:** Add the values to get 2,055.6, then divide each by the total. The shares are about 24.3%, 58.4% and 17.3%, so the second centre is most preferable. Its short travel time outweighs its smaller size because the exponent of 2 punishes distance heavily.
