The class also covered how to use AI tools responsibly for market and consumer research, which feeds into brand positioning and segmentation work.

## Market Positioning and Segmentation Strategy

### Comparing market profiles before entry

**Definition:** Before deciding how to position a brand in a new market, you study the cultural and demographic profile of the area, since this shapes what kind of product mix and brand image will work.
**Class example:** Jayanagar was described as traditional and culturally rooted, while Koramangala was described as cosmopolitan with a high migrant population, better suited to branded products and smaller SKUs.
**Business example:** This mirrors how Indian retail chains like Reliance Fresh or Nature's Basket choose different assortments and store formats for a traditional neighbourhood like Malleswaram in Bengaluru versus a cosmopolitan area like Indiranagar.
**Exam tip:** Use this comparison to explain how brand positioning must adapt to local consumer psychographics, not just demographics.

### Building customer personas for segmentation

**Definition:** A persona is a composite profile of a target customer segment, built using population density, income levels, age groups and lifestyle data, and it is a standard input for brand targeting decisions (textbook).
**Class example:** The class defined personas such as EMI-paying young professionals, senior residents, and the middle class majority income segment as key groups to analyse for the final stage of the retail site project.
**Business example:** Titan uses similar persona-based segmentation across its Tanishq, Fastrack and Titan watch brands to target different age and income groups with distinct brand identities.

### Positioning against an established competitor

**Definition:** When a smaller or newer brand enters a market dominated by a strong competitor, it must choose a differentiated positioning rather than competing head-on for the same brand promise.
**Class example:** For a new supermarket opening near D-Mart in Jayanagar, the faculty recommended positioning it as "quick, fresh, and nearby" instead of trying to match D-Mart's "bulk value" positioning.
**Business example:** This is similar to how Nature's Basket and Godrej Nature's Basket positioned themselves as premium, fresh, convenience-led stores rather than competing with D-Mart or Big Bazaar on bulk pricing.
**Remember:** Differentiated positioning against a dominant competitor should be based on a genuine consumer need the leader does not serve well, not just a different tagline.

## Hub Gravity Model for Market Share Estimation

**Definition:** The Hub Gravity Model estimates how much market share a retail location or brand outlet can capture, based on two variables: attractiveness of the store and distance decay from the consumer.
**Steps:**
1. Identify the anchor competitor in the chosen area and assign it a baseline attractiveness score (commonly 1, or 100%).
2. Assign an attractiveness score to the new brand or store based on format size, SKU mix and category (value, premium, or organic retail).
3. Use the Delphi method to have a subject matter expert set the exponents or weightage for attractiveness and distance decay.
4. Calculate estimated market share for each competing location based on these inputs.
5. Use the market share estimate to project revenue and feed this into ROI and lease decisions.
**Class example:** For a new supermarket within 50 metres of D-Mart in Jayanagar, D-Mart (anchor, attractiveness 1) was estimated to hold 50 to 60 percent market share, while the new supermarket (attractiveness 0.7 to 0.8) was estimated to capture 25 to 30 percent through proximity and cannibalisation of local shoppers.
**Business example:** Retail chains such as Trent (Star Bazaar, Zudio) use comparable gravity-based catchment analysis when deciding whether to open a new outlet close to an existing large format store, weighing footfall cannibalisation against new catchment capture.
**Exam tip:** If asked to apply a market entry or brand extension framework, mention that the Hub Gravity Model links directly to commercial decisions like signing a 10-year lease, since market share estimates drive revenue projection and break-even analysis.

### Other market share cases discussed

**Class example:** A high-opportunity area showed 44 percent potential market share because no good supermarkets existed there; a high-competition area showed only 20 percent due to density of rivals. A restaurant case in Rajouri Garden, Delhi, in a residential area, was estimated at 10 to 15 percent market share. A Surat location showed 25 to 30 percent potential, against the largest competitor holding 40 percent.
**Remember:** Market share potential is highest where competitive density is low and consumer need is unmet, even if the area is smaller.

### Data limitations and use of proxy indicators

**Definition:** When direct market or population data is unavailable or unreliable, brand and retail analysts use proxy indicators that correlate with the target customer's presence.
**Class example:** In rural Kerala, Google Maps data was noted as insufficient or inaccurate due to distributed geography. Proximity to schools was used as a proxy for family presence to site supermarkets, and proximity to hospitals was used as a proxy to site medical stores.
**Business example:** Apollo Pharmacy commonly locates new stores near hospitals and clinics for exactly this reason, using healthcare facility proximity as a demand proxy.

### Alternative data sources for site and brand analysis

**Definition:** Besides gravity models and proxies, real transaction and loyalty data can be used to validate or refine market opportunity estimates.
**Class example:** In Germany, 99 percent accurate postal data lets companies map loyalty programme members to exact addresses and spot underrepresented regions. PhonePe Pulse was cited as a B2B service providing anonymised category-wise payment data (for example, salons, clothing, supermarkets) that can reveal commercial opportunity by area.
**Business example:** PhonePe Pulse data has been publicly used by researchers and brands in India to study digital payment adoption and category spending patterns across states and cities.

## Responsible Use of AI Tools in Market and Brand Research

**Definition:** When using AI tools such as Co-pilot for demographic, psychographic or market research, you must set explicit constraints so the output is reliable enough to base brand decisions on.
**Class example:** The team agreed the AI prompt must demand authentic, cited data sources, avoid overfitting (agreeing with the user without reason), and avoid hallucinating data that does not exist.
**Business example:** Market research agencies like Nielsen and Kantar apply similar data validation discipline when they use AI-assisted tools to supplement primary research for brand studies, since unverified AI output can mislead brand positioning decisions.
**Exam tip:** If a question asks about limitations of AI in marketing research, use these three constraints (authenticity, no overfitting, no hallucination) as your answer structure.

## What the faculty stressed

- Market entry and positioning decisions must combine quantitative modelling (Hub Gravity Model) with actual field validation, since maps and models miss physical realities like highways blocking footfall.
- A new brand entering near a dominant competitor should choose a differentiated positioning built on an unmet consumer need, not try to copy the leader's model.
- Any AI-assisted research used for brand or market decisions must be constrained against hallucination and overfitting, and data sources must be authentic and cited.
- When direct data is unavailable (as in rural Kerala), use sensible proxies tied to the target customer's real behaviour, such as schools for families or hospitals for medical demand.
- Market share estimates are not just academic; they directly determine commercial decisions like signing long-term leases and calculating ROI and break-even.

## Practice questions

**Q1.** Explain the Hub Gravity Model and the two main variables it uses to estimate market share.
**A:** The Hub Gravity Model estimates market share for a retail or brand location using attractiveness and distance decay. Attractiveness depends on factors like population relevance, SKU mix and store format, while distance decay reflects the drop in ability to capture a customer as distance increases. A subject matter expert typically sets the weightage for these variables using the Delphi method. The model's output feeds directly into revenue projection and lease decisions.

**Q2.** Using the Jayanagar D-Mart case, explain how a new entrant should position itself against a dominant competitor.
**A:** In the Jayanagar case, D-Mart was the anchor competitor with high baseline attractiveness and an estimated 50 to 60 percent market share. The new supermarket, with lower initial attractiveness of 0.7 to 0.8, was advised to position itself as "quick, fresh, and nearby" rather than competing on D-Mart's bulk value model. This shows that a smaller entrant should differentiate on an unmet need, such as convenience and freshness, rather than matching the leader head-on.

**Q3.** What is a proxy indicator in market analysis, and why is it needed?
**A:** A proxy indicator is a substitute data point used when direct data about a target customer or market is unavailable or unreliable. It is needed because actual population, demand or behavioural data may be missing, as seen with rural Kerala where Google Maps data was inaccurate. Examples discussed in class include using school proximity as a proxy for family presence when siting supermarkets, and hospital proximity as a proxy for medical store demand.

**Q4.** Why is field validation still necessary even after running a quantitative site selection model?
**A:** Quantitative models like the Hub Gravity Model rely on available digital data such as maps, which may not capture every real-world constraint. The faculty gave the example of a highway that blocks foot traffic but does not show up as a barrier on a map. Field validation checks whether physical, social or infrastructural realities match the model's assumptions. Combining both back-office analysis and ground-level checks reduces the risk of a wrong site or positioning decision.

**Q5.** List and explain the three constraints the class set for using AI tools in market research.
**A:** The three constraints were authenticity, no overfitting and no hallucination. Authenticity means data sources must be real and cited rather than invented to please the user. No overfitting means the AI should not simply agree with or reinforce whatever the user suggests without independent reasoning. No hallucination means the AI must not generate data that does not actually exist, which is critical since flawed data can lead to wrong brand and market decisions.

**Q6.** How does psychographic and demographic comparison help in brand and market segmentation, using the Jayanagar versus Koramangala example?
**A:** Psychographic and demographic comparison helps identify what type of brand positioning and product mix will resonate in a given area. Jayanagar was described as traditional and culturally rooted, suggesting a fit for familiar, value-driven products, while Koramangala was described as cosmopolitan with a high migrant population, suggesting better fit for branded products and smaller SKUs. This comparison lets a brand tailor its assortment and messaging to local consumer identity rather than applying one strategy everywhere.

**Q7.** Explain why market share estimates from models like the Hub Gravity Model matter for commercial decisions such as leasing.
**A:** Market share estimates translate directly into expected revenue for a proposed location. Since retail leases are often signed for long periods, such as 10 years, an accurate market share estimate is essential before committing. This revenue projection is then used to calculate ROI and determine the break-even point for the investment. Without a reliable estimate, a brand risks committing to a long-term lease that may not be commercially viable.

**Q8.** What alternative data sources, besides map-based modelling, were discussed for validating market opportunity, and why are they useful?
**A:** The class discussed German postal data, which is 99 percent accurate and lets companies map loyalty programme members to exact addresses to find underrepresented regions, and PhonePe Pulse, a B2B service offering anonymised category-wise payment data such as spending on salons, clothing and supermarkets. These sources are useful because they are based on actual consumer transactions or verified addresses rather than estimated map data, making them more reliable for spotting real commercial opportunity.
