## Retail strategy: value and premium positioning

### Dual positioning

**Definition:** A store runs two positions at once. Value retail is the main footfall driver, and a premium range gives customers a reason to trade up once they are inside.
**Class example:** The proposed supermarket balances value retail with premium organic foods to capture a wider market area. Value pulls people in; premium organic offers an upgrade path.
**Business example:** Reliance Smart Bazaar sells low-priced staples in bulk while stocking premium and organic brands on separate shelves, so a family that comes for rice and dal may also pick up organic honey.
**Remember:** Value = traffic. Premium = margin and upgrade.

### Target personas

**Definition:** A persona is a detailed profile of a typical customer in a segment, used to shape assortment, pack sizes and promotions.
**Class example:** Value seekers are the primary segment, split into three personas:
- Budget family shoppers who buy in bulk every week and want value packs and discount loyalty programmes.
- Young tech professionals.
- Health conscious homemakers.
**Business example:** DMart's assortment and large pack sizes are built for the budget family shopper who stocks up monthly or weekly.
**Exam tip:** Link each persona to a specific part of the offer. The budget family maps to value packs; the health conscious homemaker maps to the organic range.

### SKU distribution

**Definition:** The share of shelf space and product lines given to each price tier or category.
**Class example:** 70% of SKUs for value retail and 30% for premium organic. This ratio was the decision for the proposed area.
**Business example:** Spencer's Retail repositioned towards a more premium, gourmet mix in many Kolkata stores while keeping everyday staples, adjusting the split to match its upmarket catchments.
**Formula:** SKU split = 70% value + 30% premium organic.

## Retail site selection: the Hub Gravity Model

### Fundamentals of the model

**Definition:** The Huff Gravity Model (heard in class as "Hub" model) estimates the probability that a shopper will visit a given store. It rests on two variables: the store's attractiveness and the shopper's distance from it. Attractiveness pulls customers in; distance pushes them away.
**Class example:** Attractiveness depends on population relevance, SKU mix and whether the store is value, premium or organic. Distance decay means your chance of capturing a customer falls as their distance from you rises.
**Business example:** Retail chains such as Trent (Star and Zudio) and Reliance Retail use catchment and trade area models before signing new store leases.
**Formula:** Probability of shopper i choosing store j = (A_j / D_ij^λ) ÷ Σ (A_k / D_ik^λ), where A is attractiveness, D is distance or travel time and λ is the distance decay exponent (textbook).
**Remember:** Bigger and closer wins. Smaller and farther loses.

### Delphi method for weights

**Definition:** The Delphi method uses expert opinion to assign weights. In practice, a panel of experts gives estimates over several rounds until they agree (textbook).
**Class example:** A subject matter expert, such as a marketing manager, sets the attractiveness score and the distance decay exponent from their understanding of the market.
**Business example:** A regional head at a chain like More Retail would judge how much a new format can pull compared with an existing DMart, based on years of local experience.

### Case study: supermarket next to D-Mart in Jayanagar

**Definition:** Applying the gravity model to estimate the share a new store can win against an established competitor.
**Class example:**
- Scenario: a new supermarket opens within 50 metres of a D-Mart in Jayanagar.
- D-Mart is the anchor competitor with attractiveness 1 (100%).
- The new store gets 0.7 to 0.8, since it is a smaller format and still has to build brand awareness.
- Result: D-Mart keeps 50 to 60% share; the new store can capture 25 to 30% through proximity and by pulling away local shoppers.
- Decision: position the new store as "quick, fresh and nearby" against D-Mart's "bulk value".
**Business example:** Quick-format stores such as Reliance Smart Point and neighbourhood supermarkets compete with DMart on convenience and freshness rather than price.
**Exam tip:** When a competitor is bigger, do not fight on its strength. Show how you would differentiate, as the class did with "quick, fresh, nearby".

### Commercial use of market share estimates

**Definition:** Market share estimates feed revenue projections, which feed ROI and break-even analysis.
**Class example:** These estimates are critical before you sign a long lease, such as a 10-year agreement.
**Business example:** DMart prefers to own most of its store properties, so its site decisions tie up capital for decades and need careful revenue forecasting.
**Formula:** Market share estimate → revenue projection → ROI and break-even → lease decision.

### Comparative market estimates

**Definition:** Running the same model across different sites shows which location offers more opportunity.
**Class example:**
- High opportunity area: 44% potential share, because no good supermarket exists there.
- High competition area: only 20%, due to density and heavy competition.
- Restaurant in Rajouri Garden, Delhi: 10 to 15% share in a mainly residential area.
- Surat location: 25 to 30% share, with the largest competitor holding 40%.
**Business example:** Many retailers expand into tier 2 cities like Surat and Indore because established competition is thinner there.
**Remember:** Gaps in supply (44%) beat crowded markets (20%).

## Data limitations and proxies

### Mapping challenges

**Definition:** Gravity models depend on accurate location and population data, which is not always available.
**Class example:** In Kerala, scattered settlements and geography make mapping hard, and Google Maps data was found to be weak or inaccurate for rural areas.
**Business example:** Rural-focused players like Reliance's JioMart partner kiranas rely on local distributor knowledge where digital maps are thin.

### Proxy data

**Definition:** When direct data is missing, you use an indirect indicator that tracks the thing you want to measure.
**Class example:** For supermarkets, nearness to schools is a proxy for families. For medical stores, nearness to hospitals is the main proxy.
**Business example:** Apollo Pharmacy and MedPlus place many outlets next to hospitals and clinics, where prescription demand is concentrated.
**Exam tip:** Always justify a proxy. Schools signal families with children, who are the core grocery buyers.

## Alternative site selection methods

### Loyalty and postal data

**Definition:** Mapping loyalty programme members to their home addresses to see which areas are under-served.
**Class example:** In Germany, postal data is about 99% accurate, so retailers can plot loyalty members by address and spot regions where they are under-represented.
**Business example:** Shoppers Stop uses its First Citizen loyalty data to study where its customers live and shop.

### Payment data (PhonePe Pulse)

**Definition:** Anonymised digital payment data, grouped by category and area, used to find commercial opportunity.
**Class example:** PhonePe Pulse, described as a B2B service, shows payment data by category such as salons, clothing and supermarkets.
**Business example:** PhonePe Pulse publishes UPI transaction trends by state and district, which a chain can use to see where spending in its category is growing.

## Analysis plus field work

**Definition:** Models give estimates, but you must check them on the ground before committing.
**Class example:** A map may not show physical barriers, such as a highway that stops people walking across. Good site selection combines back-office modelling with field validation.
**Business example:** Retail expansion teams at chains like Trent and DMart visit sites to count footfall and check access before finalising a location.
**Exam tip:** End any site selection answer with field validation. Examiners look for the limits of the model.

## What the faculty stressed

- Value retail drives footfall; premium organic gives the upgrade path. Keep the 70:30 SKU split in mind.
- The gravity model rests on two variables: attractiveness and distance decay.
- Weights come from expert judgement through the Delphi method.
- The Jayanagar case: D-Mart at 1, new store at 0.7 to 0.8, shares of 50 to 60% and 25 to 30%, and the "quick, fresh, nearby" positioning.
- Market share estimates are the base for revenue, ROI, break-even and long lease decisions.
- Use proxies (schools, hospitals) when data is missing, and always validate in the field.

## Practice questions

**Q1.** Explain the dual positioning strategy discussed in class.
**A:** The store uses value retail as its main footfall driver and a premium organic range as an upgrade path for customers already in the store. The class set the SKU split at 70% value and 30% premium organic. This lets the store serve budget family shoppers while also catching health conscious homemakers and young professionals.

**Q2.** What are the two variables of the Huff (Hub) Gravity Model and how do they work?
**A:** The variables are attractiveness and distance. Attractiveness depends on store size, SKU mix and format, and it increases the pull of a store. Distance decay means the chance of winning a customer falls as their distance or travel time from the store increases.

**Q3.** A new supermarket opens 50 metres from a D-Mart in Jayanagar. How would you estimate its share and position it?
**A:** Treat D-Mart as the anchor with attractiveness 1 and give the new store 0.7 to 0.8 because it is smaller and less known. The class estimate gave D-Mart 50 to 60% and the new store 25 to 30%. The new store should position itself as "quick, fresh and nearby" instead of competing with D-Mart on bulk value.

**Q4.** Why are market share estimates important before signing a lease?
**A:** Market share drives the revenue projection for the site. Revenue projections feed ROI and break-even analysis. Since leases can run for 10 years, a wrong estimate locks the retailer into a loss-making site.

**Q5.** What is proxy data in site selection? Give two examples.
**A:** Proxy data is an indirect indicator used when direct data is not available. Nearness to schools works as a proxy for families when locating a supermarket. Nearness to hospitals is the main proxy for a medical store.

**Q6.** Why must gravity model results be checked through field work?
**A:** Models depend on map data, which can be wrong or incomplete, as seen in rural Kerala. Maps also miss physical barriers like a highway that blocks foot traffic. Combining back-office modelling with on-ground validation avoids costly location mistakes.
