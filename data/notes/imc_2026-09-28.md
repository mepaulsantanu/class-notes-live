## Advertising Fundamentals: Copy, Illustrations, and Brand Elements

### Copy vs body copy
**Definition:** Copy is everything in the ad layout shown to the client before it goes to media, while body copy is only the written text within the ad.
**Class example:** A minimalistic ad can have heavy white space and no body copy at all, but the layout itself is still called copy.
**Business example:** Fevicol print ads often use minimal body copy but rely on a strong visual, yet the whole print layout is still "copy" in advertising terms.

### Illustrations
**Definition:** Illustrations are the pictorial, non-verbal elements of an advertisement.
**Class example:** Photographs, cartoons, drawings, and AI-generated images were all given as valid illustration types.
**Business example:** Amul's cartoon mascot in its topical ads is a classic Indian illustration-led campaign.

### Essential brand elements
**Definition:** Illustrations and taglines are optional in an ad, but the brand name and logo must always be present.
**Class example:** A brand was discussed where the name and logo are one and the same, with no separate symbol attached.
**Business example:** Coca-Cola's script logo is both its name and its visual identity, similar to the example discussed in class.
**Remember:** Brand name and logo are non-negotiable in any ad; everything else is flexible.

## Marketing Communication Frameworks: ATL, BTL, TTL

**Definition:**
1. ATL (Above the Line): mass media, broad-reach, often government-regulated advertising channels like TV and print.
2. BTL (Below the Line): targeted, direct communication methods aimed at specific consumer groups.
3. TTL (Through the Line): a combined strategy using both ATL and BTL together.

**Class example:** These three were defined as the core categories of product and marketing communication.
**Business example:** Hindustan Unilever uses ATL television ads for mass brand building (like Surf Excel) alongside BTL activities such as in-store sampling, making it a TTL strategy overall.
**Exam tip:** Always define all three together in a comparative table if asked; examiners like to see the contrast.

## Silent Communication Framework

**Definition:** Silent communication is the non-verbal interaction between a product and a potential buyer, occurring in a fixed three-step sequence before a purchase decision is made.

**Steps (must follow this order):**
1. Visual communication: the product's look, such as brand, color, and design, attracts the customer's eyes.
2. Tactile communication: the customer touches and feels the product to assess quality.
3. Performance communication: the customer checks how the product actually functions or looks in use.

**Class example:** In a garment store, a customer is first drawn to a shirt by its color and design (visual), then touches the fabric to check quality (tactile), then tries it on to check fit (performance).

**Business example:** Zara stores are designed for this exact sequence, encouraging customers to see, touch, and try garments in-store before buying.

### E-commerce disruption of the sequence
**Definition:** Online shopping often breaks the natural visual-tactile-performance order.
**Class example:** On e-commerce sites, customers see the product (visual) but skip tactile and performance stages until after delivery, leading to returns if fit or look is unsatisfactory.
**Business example:** Myntra and Amazon Fashion face high return rates because customers can only judge tactile and performance quality after the product physically arrives.
**Exam tip:** This is a good point to raise in any question about challenges of digital retail versus physical retail.

## Case Discussion: Marketing Jewelry to Children

**Definition:** The class debated whether it is appropriate to position and advertise a jewelry product specifically for eight-year-old children.

**Points raised in favour:**
- In some cultures, such as Bengali culture, children commonly wear jewelry, so the positioning feels normal.
- Children are maturing faster today, making them a more viable consumer segment.

**Concerns raised:**
- Children are easily influenced by advertising and may pressure parents into buying.
- Advertising to children can increase peer pressure among young consumers.
- Traditional advertising logic advises against targeting children directly or using children as characters in ads, though it was noted that this "old world logic" may not fully apply to modern markets.

**Business example:** Indian jewelry brands like Tanishq have occasionally faced public debate over how they represent family and children in ad campaigns, similar to the ethical concerns raised in class.
**Exam tip:** If asked about ethics in advertising, use this case to discuss the tension between market opportunity and responsible advertising to vulnerable audiences.

## Practice questions

**Q1.** Differentiate between copy and body copy in advertising, with an example.
**A:** Copy refers to the entire ad layout, including all visual and textual elements shown to the client before media release. Body copy is only the written text within that layout. A minimalistic ad can have no body copy at all and still rely heavily on white space, but the full layout is still called copy.

**Q2.** Explain the three-step silent communication sequence between a product and a customer.
**A:** Silent communication happens in a fixed order: visual, tactile, and then performance. Visual communication is when the product's look, such as color or design, first attracts the customer. Tactile communication follows when the customer touches the product to judge quality, and performance communication is the final stage where the customer checks how the product functions or looks in actual use, such as trying on a garment.

**Q3.** How does e-commerce disrupt the traditional silent communication sequence, and what is the business impact?
**A:** In e-commerce, customers only get the visual stage before purchase, since they cannot touch or try the product online. The tactile and performance stages happen only after delivery, which often leads to dissatisfaction and returns if fit or feel does not match expectations. This is why platforms like Myntra face high return rates compared to physical retail.

**Q4.** Differentiate between ATL, BTL, and TTL communication with a business example.
**A:** ATL refers to mass media advertising through broad-reach channels like TV and print. BTL refers to targeted, direct methods such as in-store promotions. TTL combines both strategies together. Hindustan Unilever's Surf Excel uses ATL television campaigns along with BTL in-store sampling, making its overall strategy TTL.

**Q5.** Name any two Indian advertising legends and describe their key contribution.
**A:** Alyque Padamsee is regarded as the father of Indian advertising and was associated with the agency Lintas. Piyush Pandey, a legendary figure at Ogilvy, is known for iconic Cadbury campaigns and wrote the autobiography "Pandemonium" with a foreword by Amitabh Bachchan.

**Q6.** What are the fixed constraints and creative freedoms in the Usha CIA assignment?
**A:** The fixed constraints are that the brand name must stay Usha, the product must remain a sewing machine, the punchline must stay "Can't buy better," and both the product and a human character must be visible in the final image. Creative freedom exists for headline, body copy, illustration style, character choice, perspective (such as feminist angles), and setting (domestic or corporate).

**Q7.** Discuss the ethical debate around advertising jewelry products to children, as raised in class.
**A:** The class debated whether jewelry aimed at eight-year-olds is appropriate, noting that in some cultures like Bengali culture this is culturally normal and children today mature faster as consumers. However, concerns were raised that children are easily influenced by ads and can create peer pressure or pester power. Traditional advertising ethics advise against targeting children directly, though it was acknowledged that this old-world logic may not fully apply in today's market.

**Q8.** What is an advertising campaign, and give an example discussed in class.
**A:** An advertising campaign is a series of advertisements that share a common theme, usually expressed through a consistent slogan. Prasoon Joshi's "Thanda Matlab Coca-Cola" campaign for McCann Erickson is a real example discussed in class of a theme-driven, slogan-based campaign.
